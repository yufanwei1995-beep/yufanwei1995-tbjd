Buyer's Guide: What to Look For

Don't buy a bed based on the picture. Buy it based on the foam density and the washability. Here is what actually matters for an anxious, arthritic dog.

1. Foam Density (Measured in lbs/ft³)

Arthritic dogs need support foam, not memory foam. Memory foam feels soft to your hand but lets the dog sink too deep. Look for high-density polyurethane foam rated at 2.5 lbs/ft³ or higher. The Big Barker uses 3.0 lbs/ft³. If the listing doesn't specify the density, assume it's cheap filler.

2. Bolster Height and Shape

Anxiety relief comes from the "cuddle curl" effect. The bolster (the raised edge) needs to be tall enough to rest the dog's head, but not so tall it blocks their line of sight. A J-shaped bolster is better than a full circle because it allows easy entry for stiff joints. Avoid beds with floppy bolsters—they don't provide the pressure that calms a nervous dog.

3. Bed Height (The "Get Up" Factor)

This is critical for arthritis. A bed that is too low forces the dog to strain their hips to stand. A bed that is too high makes them jump. For medium to large dogs, look for a bed height of 4 to 7 inches. This reduces the angle of the hip joint when they stand, reducing pain spikes.

4. Washability

Anxious dogs drool or sweat more. Arthritic dogs often have accidents. The cover needs to be fully removable with a zipper that goes around the entire perimeter. Look for a waterproof liner underneath the cover. If you can't throw the cover in a washing machine, skip it.

5. Heat Retention

Arthritis causes inflammation, which makes joints hot. A bed that traps body heat will make the dog restless. Gel-infused foam or a breathable cotton canvas cover is essential. Avoid faux suede or fleece—they feel cozy but cook your dog.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a bed actually reduce anxiety, or is it just marketing?

Yes, but not by itself. The "calming" effect comes from the bolster applying gentle, even pressure—similar to a weighted blanket. If the bed has a tall, firm bolster, it reduces startle responses. If the bolster is flimsy, it does nothing. Look for beds with a distinct, firm raised edge, not just a tufted pillow.

How often should I replace the bed for an arthritic dog?

Every 12 to 18 months. Even good foam loses density. A quick test: press your hand into the center. If you see a permanent indentation that takes more than 10 seconds to rebound, the bed is dead. Sleeping on a compressed bed worsens joint pain.

My dog has hip dysplasia. Should I get a flat or bolster bed?

Get a bolster bed, but make sure the bolster is J-shaped (open on one side). A full circle forces the dog to step over the edge to get in, which strains the hip abductors. An open side lets them walk straight in and turn around.

Is a heated bed better for arthritis?

No. Heat reduces stiffness temporarily, but it increases inflammation over time. Anxious dogs also tend to overheat. Instead of a heated bed, look for a cooling gel layer or a breathable cotton cover. You can always add a separate heating pad for cold nights.

What if my dog is a chewer?

Skip all of these. The covers are not chew-proof. If your dog chews on beds, you need a raised cot-style bed (like the Kuranda) with a chew-proof fabric. But understand that a cot doesn't provide the same joint support. You may need to treat the anxiety first with a behaviorist before investing in an expensive bed.
