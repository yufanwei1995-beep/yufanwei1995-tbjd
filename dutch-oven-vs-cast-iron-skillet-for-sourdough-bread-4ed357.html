Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters for Sourdough

Before we get into specific products, you need to understand the four things that control your loaf quality. Ignore marketing hype and focus on these.

1. Thermal Mass (The Heavy Stuff)

Sourdough needs a blast of heat to spring up in the oven. Heavy cast iron holds heat and releases it evenly. A thin stainless pan loses heat when the dough hits it, giving you a flat puck. Look for pans weighing at least 5 pounds. Heavier is better, period.

2. Steam Trapping

Steam keeps the dough surface gelatinized during the first 15 minutes of baking. That's what gives you a glossy, crackly crust. A dutch oven with a tight lid traps the water released from the dough itself. A skillet can't do that on its own. You'll need a metal pan of water on the rack below, and even then, you lose steam when you open the oven.

3. Shape and Depth

Dutch ovens are deep, which supports a round boule and forces the dough upward. Skillets are shallow and wide, so dough spreads sideways. You can get a nice oval or batard shape in a skillet, but you'll never get the same vertical rise. If you want tall, round loaves, get a dutch oven.

4. Preheating Time

Both pans need a full preheat at 450-500°F for at least 45 minutes. A dutch oven takes longer to heat through because of its depth. A skillet heats faster because it's thinner. But the dutch oven holds the heat better when the lid comes off. Factor this into your morning routine.

Product Reviews: I Tested These Four Pans

I tested four pans over three weeks. Same dough (80% hydration, 20% whole wheat), same oven (a basic electric model), same scoring pattern. Results were consistent across multiple bakes.

1. Lodge 6-Quart Cast Iron Dutch Oven

Price: $50-$70 | Rating: 5/5

This is the gold standard for a reason. The 6-quart size fits a standard boule with room to spare. The cast iron is thick — 0.25 inches at the walls — and it holds heat like a bank vault. I preheated it for 50 minutes at 475°F, dropped in my dough, and the oven spring was dramatic. The loaf rose about 3 inches taller than the skillet versions.

The lid is heavy (the whole thing weighs about 13 pounds), and it seals well enough to trap steam. My crust came out deep brown, glossy, and thin. The ear where I scored was crisp and shattered when I tapped it.

The tradeoff: it's awkward to handle when hot. The handles are small and you'll need thick mitts. Also, the enameled version (about $90) is easier to clean, but the bare cast iron version needs regular seasoning to avoid rust.

Best for: Bakers who want consistent, foolproof results with minimal fuss.

2. Lodge 12-Inch Cast Iron Skillet

Price: $25-$35 | Rating: 3.5/5

The classic workhorse. It's cheaper, lighter (about 8 pounds), and heats up faster than the dutch oven. For sourdough, it produces a wide, flat loaf — more like a focaccia-meets-boule hybrid. My test loaf spread to about 10 inches across and only rose 2 inches. The crust was thicker and chewier, closer to artisan country bread.

To get steam, I placed a metal loaf pan filled with water on the rack below. It worked, but the steam dissipated every time I opened the oven to check. The crust was less glossy than the dutch oven version. It's also harder to transfer dough into a shallow pan — the dough wants to flop over the edge. I used a parchment sling and it helped, but it's still fiddly.

The tradeoff: you save $30 but sacrifice height and crust quality. If you already own one, it's worth trying. If you're buying new, spend the extra cash.

Best for: Budget bakers or those who want a multi-purpose pan for frying and roasting too.

3. Le Creuset 5.5-Quart Enameled Dutch Oven

Price: $300-$350 | Rating: 4.5/5

This is the luxury option, and it performs beautifully. The enameled interior means no seasoning, no rust, and easy cleanup — a quick wipe with soapy water and it's done. The 5.5-quart size is slightly smaller than the Lodge, but it was still plenty for a 1kg loaf.

Performance-wise, it matched the Lodge on oven spring and crust. The enameled surface releases the dough better than bare cast iron, so I didn't need as much flour on my parchment. The wide handles are easier to grip with mitts, and the lid fits tighter than the Lodge's, which meant slightly better steam retention.

The tradeoff is the price. At $300+, this is three times the Lodge. You're paying for aesthetics and durability — these pans last decades. But for sourdough specifically, the Lodge produces identical results for a fraction of the cost.

Best for: People who want a beautiful pan that serves as both bread oven and serving dish, and who don't mind the premium.

4. Victoria 10.25-Inch Cast Iron Skillet

Price: $20-$30 | Rating: 3/5

I included this because it's the cheapest option out there and I wanted to see if it could compete. It's lighter than the Lodge skillet (about 7 pounds) and has a smooth cooking surface that's pre-seasoned with flaxseed oil. It heats evenly, but the thinner walls mean more temperature fluctuation when the cold dough hits.

My loaf was the flattest of all four — barely 1.5 inches tall. The crust was tough, almost leathery, because the steam evaporated too quickly. The smooth surface also meant the dough slid around more when I tried to score it in the pan.

The tradeoff: it's a fine pan for eggs and cornbread, but it's not ideal for sourdough. You can make it work with a steam tray and lower hydration dough, but you're fighting the physics.

Best for: Strict budget buyers who already have this pan and want to try sourdough before investing more.

FAQ: Your Questions Answered

Can I use a glass or ceramic dish instead?

No. Glass and ceramic can't handle the preheat temperature. Most bakeware is only rated to 450°F, and they can shatter from thermal shock when the cold dough hits. Stick with cast iron or heavy stainless steel.

Do I need a dutch oven if I have a steam-injected oven?

If your oven has a steam injection feature, a skillet works much better. The steam does the work the lid would normally do. But most home ovens don't have this — check your manual. If you're just putting a pan of water on the bottom rack, that's not enough for a good crust.

What about the Lodge combo cooker?

The combo cooker is a skillet and shallow dutch oven lid that doubles as a pan. It's a great middle ground — you get the steam of a dutch oven with the low profile of a skillet. It costs about $40 and is worth considering if you want flexibility.

How do I prevent the dough from sticking?

Use a parchment sling. Cut a 12-inch square, place the dough on it, and lower it into the hot pan. No need for oil or flour. The parchment handles 500°F without burning.

The Bottom Line

Buy the Lodge 6-Quart Cast Iron Dutch Oven. It's the best balance of price and performance for sourdough bread. It traps steam, holds heat, and gives you that crackly, glossy crust you're after. The skillet works in a pinch — if you already own one, try it with a steam tray. But if you're buying new gear, the dutch oven is the right tool for the job. The Le Creuset is nicer to look at and easier to clean, but it won't bake a better loaf. Save the $250 and spend it on good flour.
