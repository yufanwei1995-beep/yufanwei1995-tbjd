Quick Summary

Who this is for: Anyone recovering from knee surgery, patellar tendinitis, arthritis, or general joint pain who wants a low-impact cardio machine that won't flare up their knees.

Our top pick: NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle — the auto-adjusting incline and forward-leaning geometry are easier on sensitive knees than Peloton's aggressive race posture.

Price range: Peloton Bike ($1,445) vs NordicTrack S22i ($1,999). Both often go on sale — we've seen the S22i drop to $1,499.

Knee pain and cycling is a tricky mix. Done right, it's one of the best rehab tools you can use. Done wrong, it'll set you back weeks. The pedal stroke is smooth and non-impact, which makes both the Peloton Bike and the NordicTrack S22i solid options for recovery. But they're very different machines with very different geometries, resistance systems, and price tags.

I've spent the last month alternating between both bikes with a focus on knee joint stress. I've also consulted with a physical therapist who specializes in post-op knee rehab to understand what actually matters when you're pedaling through pain. Here's what I found.

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For When You Have Knee Pain

Before you drop serious money on a stationary bike, understand these five factors. They matter more than the screen size or the class library.

1. Frame Geometry (The Most Important Factor)

This is the difference between a bike that feels like a road racer and one that feels like a cruiser. A more upright, relaxed geometry (like the S22i) puts your hips at a more open angle, which reduces pressure on the patellofemoral joint — the spot right behind your kneecap that hurts for most people. A more aggressive, leaned-forward position (like the Peloton) increases quad engagement but also increases compressive forces on the kneecap.

The rule: If your pain is behind the kneecap (chondromalacia, runner's knee), go upright. If your pain is in the joint line or IT band, either bike can work with proper fit.

2. Resistance Type

Peloton uses magnetic resistance — silent, smooth, and infinitely adjustable. The S22i also uses magnetic resistance but adds a motorized incline/decline that shifts your body weight. Both are much better for knees than friction-based resistance (like the old-school spin bikes) because there's no jerky engagement.

3. Crank Arm Length

This is a spec nobody talks about. Peloton's crank arms are 170mm. The S22i's are also 170mm. That's standard, but if you have limited knee flexion (you can't bend your knee past 90 degrees comfortably), you'd ideally want 165mm cranks. Neither bike offers that stock. You'd need to swap them out — a $50-80 modification that's totally worth it if your range of motion is restricted.

4. Seat and Handlebar Adjustability

Both bikes adjust fore/aft and up/down on the seat and handlebars. But the S22i's handlebars have a few more positions, letting you find a truly neutral spine angle. Peloton's handlebars are fine, but the reach is longer, which pulls you forward into that aggressive position.

5. The Screen and Programming

Neither bike's screen helps your knees directly, but the programming matters. Peloton's classes are highly structured and push you toward output goals. That's a double-edged sword — you might push too hard and flare up your knee. NordicTrack's iFit programming includes low-impact rides that specifically target joint health, and the auto-adjusting resistance takes the guesswork out of pacing.

Product Reviews

Peloton Bike (Original)

Price: $1,445 (bike only) | Rating: 4/5 for knee recovery

The original Peloton Bike is a beautifully built machine. The magnetic resistance is butter-smooth, and the 21.5-inch touchscreen (on the newer models) is crisp and responsive. The classes are the best in the industry — no question. The instructors are motivating, and the leaderboard adds a competitive element that keeps you coming back.

But here's the problem for knee recovery: the geometry. The Peloton's design is inspired by road racing. The handlebars sit lower and further forward than the S22i. When you're in that position, your hip flexors tighten, and your quads take on more load. For someone with patellofemoral pain, that's exactly what you don't want.

You can mitigate this by raising the handlebars all the way and moving the seat forward, but the bike's frame limits how upright you can get. I measured the maximum handlebar height at 37.5 inches from the floor — that's fine for most people, but taller riders (6'0" and up) will find it cramped.

The good: The resistance is quiet, the classes are addictive, and the resale value is strong. The 12-week "Starting Out" program is great for building a base without overdoing it.

The bad: The aggressive posture can aggravate kneecap pain. Also, the membership is $44/month, and the bike is essentially a brick without it — you can't view any stats or take recorded classes without paying.

Best for: People whose knee pain is mild or joint-line specific, and who prioritize class quality over comfort.

NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle

Price: $1,999 (often on sale for $1,499) | Rating: 4.5/5 for knee recovery

The S22i is the bike I'd pick for knee recovery, and it's not particularly close. The key differentiator is the 20% incline and 10% decline. When you're in a decline (handlebars lower than the seat), you shift more weight onto your hands and off your knees. When you're in an incline, you engage your glutes more and take pressure off the patellar tendon.

More importantly, the frame geometry is more upright than the Peloton. I measured the handlebar height at 41 inches — 3.5 inches taller than the Peloton. That extra height lets you sit back and pedal with a neutral spine, which opens up your hip angle and reduces compressive forces on the kneecap.

The auto-adjusting resistance is another win. In an iFit class, the trainer controls the resistance and incline for you. For someone in pain, that means you can't accidentally crank up the resistance to a level that hurts. You still have manual override, but the auto mode is genuinely helpful for pacing.

The good: The incline/decline is a game-changer for finding a pain-free position. The 22-inch rotating screen is huge. The 30-day iFit trial is generous, and the family membership ($39/month) covers everyone.

The bad: The bike is heavier (205 lbs vs Peloton's 135 lbs) and harder to move. The iFit integration can be glitchy — I had to restart the screen twice in my test month. Also, some users report the seat is uncomfortable for longer rides, so budget for a gel cover.

Best for: Anyone with patellofemoral pain, recent knee surgery, or arthritis who wants the most comfortable, adjustable riding position.

Peloton Bike+

Price: $2,495 | Rating: 3.5/5 for knee recovery

The Bike+ is Peloton's premium offering. It has a larger 23.8-inch screen, auto-follow resistance that syncs with classes, and a swivel screen for off-bike workouts. The auto-follow feature is a step toward the S22i's convenience, but it's not the same — it adjusts resistance based on the class plan, not your physical state.

Here's the kicker: the Bike+ has the same frame geometry as the original Peloton. The same 170mm cranks, the same handlebar height range, the same aggressive posture. So you're paying an extra $1,050 for a better screen and auto-follow, not for better knee comfort. If your knees are the priority, that money is better spent on the S22i.

The good: The screen is amazing. The auto-follow resistance is cool. The sound system is genuinely impressive.

The bad: Same knee-unfriendly geometry as the original. No incline/decline. The price is hard to justify for recovery purposes.

Best for: Peloton loyalists who want the best screen but don't have significant knee issues.

FAQ

Can I ride a stationary bike with a torn meniscus?

Yes, but only after the acute phase (first 48-72 hours). Cycling is non-impact and keeps the joint lubricated. Start with zero resistance and a low cadence (60-70 RPM) for 10 minutes. If pain increases, stop. The S22i's upright position is better for meniscus tears because it reduces rotational stress on the joint.

How long should I ride each day for knee recovery?

Start with 10-15 minutes at a very easy pace. The goal is to get blood flow and synovial fluid moving, not to break a sweat. Gradually add 5 minutes per week. You should not feel any sharp pain — a mild ache that goes away within an hour is acceptable.

Is the Peloton's resistance too hard for knees?

The resistance itself isn't the problem — it's how you use it. The issue is that Peloton's classes are gamified, and the leaderboard encourages you to push harder than you should. If you're disciplined and stay in "recovery" zones (low resistance, high cadence), the Peloton is fine. But if you're competitive, the S22i's auto-resistance prevents you from overdoing it.

Should I get a bike with incline/decline for knee pain?

Not necessarily, but it helps. The decline position (handlebars lower) takes weight off the saddle and reduces hip flexion, which directly reduces patellofemoral compression. If you have kneecap pain, the S22i's decline feature is genuinely therapeutic. If your pain is more joint-line or ligament-related, you can get by with a flat bike like the Peloton.

Can I use these bikes if I'm overweight?

Yes. Both bikes have a 300-lb weight capacity. The S22i's heavier frame (205 lbs) feels more stable, especially at higher cadences. The Peloton is lighter but still sturdy. The S22i's wider seat is also more comfortable for larger riders.

Bottom Line Verdict

If your knees are the deciding factor — and they should be — get the NordicTrack Commercial S22i. The upright geometry, the 20% incline/10% decline, and the auto-resistance make it the only bike of the three that's genuinely designed with joint health in mind. Yes, the iFit software can be glitchy and the bike is heavy, but those are minor annoyances compared to the comfort difference you'll feel in your knees. The Peloton Bike is a fine machine, but it's a race bike at heart — and race bikes and recovering knees don't mix well. If you're patient, wait for the S22i to go on sale (it regularly drops to $1,499), and spend the money you save on a gel seat cover and a good pair of cycling shoes. Your knees will thank you.
