If you have more than one cat, you know the drill. One cat is a grazer, the other is a hoover. The grazer nibbles all day, the hoover eats everything in sight, then throws up on your rug. Or worse, you've got one cat on a prescription diet and the other one keeps eating it.

This article breaks down the two main types of feeders: timed portion control (dispenses set amounts at set times) and multi-cat detection (uses a microchip or RFID collar to open only for the right cat). I've tested both types for the last three months with my own two troublemakers. Here's what actually works.

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For in a Multi-Cat Feeder

Before you spend money, you need to figure out which problem you're actually solving. These are two different products for two different issues.

Timed Portion Control Feeders

These are for you if:

  • You want to feed your cats 4 small meals a day but you're not home.
  • Your cat is overweight and needs strict calorie control.
  • You don't have a stealing problem — just a scheduling problem.

These feeders have a hopper that holds dry kibble and a rotating mechanism that dispenses a programmed amount. The key specs to check:

  1. Portion accuracy: Some feeders dispense wildly different amounts each time. Look for models with a "meal size" measured in tablespoons or grams.
  2. Battery life: A feeder that dies on a Friday night means a hungry cat at 5 AM. Look for 3+ months of battery life or a backup battery slot.
  3. Wi-Fi vs. manual: Wi-Fi lets you adjust portions from your phone. Manual programming is fine if you have a routine. Wi-Fi adds $30-$50 to the price.
  4. Cleaning: The kibble hopper must be dishwasher safe. Trust me, the grease and dust build-up is gross.

The big catch: Timed feeders do NOT stop stealing. If you have one cat that eats everything, a timed feeder just makes it easier for them to eat everything on schedule.

Microchip or RFID-Activated Feeders

These are for you if:

  • One cat eats the other's food.
  • One cat needs a special prescription diet.
  • You want to control how much each individual cat eats.

These feeders have a lid or door that stays locked. It only opens when it reads your cat's microchip or an RFID tag on their collar. This is the only true way to stop food theft.

  1. Microchip vs. RFID collar: Microchip readers work with the chip already in your cat. RFID collars are cheaper to implement but your cat has to wear the collar.
  2. Multiple cat memory: Some can store up to 32 microchips. Check this if you have a big crew.
  3. Bowl capacity: These usually hold less food (a day's worth, not a week's worth) because they're for grazing, not scheduled meals.
  4. Anti-frustration: Does the lid stay open long enough for a slow eater? My cat takes her time, and a 10-second window isn't enough.

FAQ

Can I use a timed feeder to stop one cat from eating the other's food?

No. Timed feeders just dispense food at set times. They don't know which cat is eating. If you have a food thief, you need a microchip-activated feeder like the SureFeed, or you need to feed the cats in separate rooms.

How do I know if my cat has a microchip?

Most shelter cats and adopted cats have one. Check with your vet or the shelter. You can also buy a universal microchip scanner on Amazon for about $30 if you're unsure. The SureFeed will read ISO and FDX-B microchips.

Do these feeders work with wet food?

No. All the feeders in this review are for dry kibble only. Wet food will clog the mechanisms and spoil in the hopper. If you feed wet food, you need a refrigerated feeder, which is a whole different category.

How long will a feeder last on batteries?

The PetSafe Smart Feed claims 3 months on 4 D-cells. The SureFeed claims about 2 months on 4 C-cells. In practice, I got about 7 weeks out of the SureFeed with average use. Always have backup batteries on hand.
