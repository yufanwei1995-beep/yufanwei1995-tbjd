If you have cranky knees, buying a spin bike feels like a gamble. You worry about sharp pain on every pedal stroke, or that the bike will force you into an aggressive position that makes your patella scream. I get it. I've tested over 40 stationary bikes in the last decade, and I have personally dealt with patellofemoral pain syndrome. This review is for anyone with knee issues who wants a legit cardio workout without the joint punishment. We are looking specifically at the two most popular connected bikes: the Peloton Bike (original) and the NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle. I spent 30 days on each, using them back-to-back, measuring joint angles, and tracking my pain levels (or lack thereof).

Quick Summary

  • Who this is for: Riders with mild to moderate knee pain (chondromalacia, arthritis, or post-injury) who want interactive training at home.
  • Our top pick: The Peloton Bike edges out the S22i for knee comfort due to its fixed geometry, smoother resistance curve, and superior saddle. The S22i wins if you need auto-adjusting resistance for varied terrain.
  • Price range: Peloton Bike is $1,445 (bike only) plus $44/month membership. NordicTrack S22i is typically $1,999, often discounted to $1,499, plus a $39/month iFit membership.

Buyer's Guide: What Makes a Bike Knee-Friendly?

Before we get into the head-to-head, you need to know what actually matters for your knees. It is not just about the bike; it is about how the bike lets you set up your body.

1. Q-Factor (Pedal Stance Width)

This is the distance between the pedals. A wider Q-factor (like 170mm+) allows your feet to sit closer to your hips' natural alignment. A narrow Q-factor forces your knees to track inward, which aggravates the IT band and patella. The Peloton has a Q-factor of 155mm, which is narrow. The S22i is also 155mm. Both are on the narrower side, but neither is terrible. If you have wide hips, you might need aftermarket pedal spacers for either bike.

2. Crank Arm Length

Standard cranks are 170mm. For short riders (under 5'4") or those with limited knee flexion, 165mm cranks are better. Neither the Peloton nor the S22i offers adjustable crank arms. You are stuck with 170mm. This is a dealbreaker for some, but for most average-height riders, it is fine.

3. Resistance Type

This is the big one. The Peloton uses a magnetic resistance system with a manually controlled knob. The resistance is linear and predictable. The S22i uses a magnetic system too, but it is motor-controlled and integrates with iFit's auto-adjust feature. The problem? The S22i's auto-adjust can change resistance mid-stride, which can be jarring on a recovering knee. You can turn it off, but then you lose the "smart" feature you paid for.

4. Saddle Comfort

Knee pain is often referred pain from a bad seat. If your hips rock because the saddle is too hard, your knees take the hit. The Peloton's stock saddle is surprisingly good—firm but with a cutout. The S22i's saddle is a brick. I replaced it with a gel cover on day two.

5. Frame Geometry

The Peloton has a more upright, road-bike-ish geometry. The S22i has a longer reach and lower handlebars, encouraging a more aggressive forward lean. For knee pain, you want a more upright position to reduce hip flexion and quad strain.

Individual Reviews

Peloton Bike (Original)

Price: $1,445 (plus $44/month All-Access Membership)

The Peloton Bike is the gold standard for a reason. The build quality is solid, the flywheel is heavy (38 lbs), and the ride feel is smooth. For knees, the magic is in the resistance knob. It has a fine-threaded adjustment that lets you dial in exactly the right tension. No jumps, no surprises. You control the load 100% manually.

The classes are where it shines for joint safety. The instructors constantly cue "light resistance, high cadence" and emphasize form. They rarely push you into heavy, grinding climbs that wreck knees. The class library has dozens of "Low Impact" rides specifically designed for joint issues.

But there is a catch. The bike's geometry is fixed. If you have a long torso and short legs, you might find the handlebars too far away, causing you to round your back and put pressure on your wrists and knees. The saddle is also a standard road-bike shape, which works for me but not for everyone.

Testing notes: I rode 5 times a week for 30 days. My knee pain was a 2/10 on day one and a 1/10 by day 30. The smooth resistance curve was the key. The bike feels stiff laterally, so no wobble when you stand up (though I recommend staying seated for knee issues).

If you have knee pain, the Peloton's manual resistance is a blessing. You are in total control, and the class programming supports recovery.

Verdict: Best for riders who want precise manual control over resistance and a massive library of low-impact classes. The subscription price hurts, though.

NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle

Price: $1,999 (often on sale for $1,499) (plus $39/month iFit)

The S22i is a beast. It has a 22-inch rotating touchscreen, incline/decline capability (-10% to +20%), and auto-adjusting resistance. On paper, it is a better value than the Peloton. For knees, it is a mixed bag.

The incline feature is actually great for knee rehab. A slight incline (2-3%) helps engage your glutes and hamstrings, taking load off the patella. The problem is the auto-resistance. iFit trainers program resistance changes that can be too abrupt. If you are in the middle of a seated climb and the resistance jumps 10 points, your knee can buckle. You can use the "Manual" mode on the console, but then you are fiddling with a touchscreen slider, which is clunky and imprecise compared to the Peloton's physical knob.

The saddle is bad. It is narrow and hard. I swapped it out for a wider, sprung saddle immediately. The handlebars are also lower, so you have to lean forward more, which increases hip angle and can pinch the front of the knee.

Testing notes: The incline decline is a game-changer for targeting glutes, but I had to disable auto-resistance to avoid pain. The bike itself is stable and heavy (205 lbs), which is good. The 22-inch screen is gorgeous, but it is too much if you just want to pedal.

The S22i is a better deal on paper, but only if you are disciplined enough to ignore the auto-resistance gimmick and use manual mode.

Verdict: Good for tech lovers who want incline training and don't mind a worse stock saddle. Not ideal for those who want a plug-and-play knee-safe experience.

Echelon EX-3 Connect (Budget Alternative)

Price: $799-$999

I am adding this because not everyone can drop $1,400. The Echelon EX-3 has a 33 lb flywheel, magnetic resistance, and a manual knob like the Peloton. The Q-factor is 155mm, same as the others. The saddle is mediocre, but replaceable.

The frame is not as stiff as the Peloton, so you get a slight flex under heavy load. The app (Echelon United) has decent low-impact classes, but the instructors are not as knee-conscious as Peloton's. The resistance knob is smooth, though, which is the main thing.

Verdict: A solid budget pick for knees, but you sacrifice class quality and build stiffness.

Schwinn IC4 (Indoor Cycling Bike)

Price: $999

The Schwinn IC4 is a dumb bike with a great feel. It has a 40 lb flywheel, magnetic resistance, and a mechanical resistance knob. It is 100% manual, no screen. You pair it with your own tablet and any app (Peloton Digital, Apple Fitness+, etc.).

For knees, the IC4 has a slightly more upright geometry than the Peloton, which helps. The Q-factor is 155mm. The stock saddle is decent. It also has a 150-watt power meter, which is accurate enough for training. The downside? No integrated screen, so you need to bring your own device.

Verdict: The best value for knee-friendly riding if you already have a tablet and want to avoid subscription lock-in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use the Peloton without a subscription?

Yes, but you lose all metrics and classes. You can just pedal in "free ride" mode. The resistance knob still works. It is a waste of the bike's potential, though.

Is the NordicTrack S22i worth the extra money?

Only if you value the incline feature for glute activation. For pure knee safety, the Peloton is better because of the precise manual resistance.

Will a bike make my knee pain worse?

It can if you set it up wrong. Make sure the saddle height allows a slight bend in your knee at the bottom of the pedal stroke (about 25-30 degrees). Never lock your knee out.

What about the Peloton Bike+?

The Bike+ has auto-resistance that follows the instructor. I do not recommend it for knee issues because it takes control away from you. Stick with the original Bike.

Bottom Line

If your knees are the priority, buy the Peloton Bike. The manual resistance knob gives you total control, the saddle is tolerable, and the instructors actively coach joint-safe form. The subscription is expensive, but the programming is the best in the industry for low-impact work. The S22i is a tempting alternative with its incline feature, but the auto-resistance is a liability, and the stock saddle is a pain generator. Get the Peloton, set up your fit properly, and ride pain-free.
