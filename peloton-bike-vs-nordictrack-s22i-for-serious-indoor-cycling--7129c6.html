Who This Is For and What Problem It Solves

You are not a casual rider. You want to get faster, build power, and track every watt. You are stuck choosing between two of the biggest names in connected fitness: the Peloton Bike and the NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle. Both cost serious money. Both promise a better workout than a cheap spinner. But they take completely different paths to get you there.

This review cuts through the marketing noise. I have spent weeks training on both bikes, testing the resistance curves, the metric accuracy, and the subscription ecosystems. I will tell you exactly which bike fits your training style, and which one will frustrate you if you buy it for the wrong reasons.

Quick Summary

Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters for Serious Training

Before diving into the head-to-head, you need to understand the four pillars of a serious indoor training bike. Ignore the marketing fluff and focus on these specs.

1. Resistance Type and Realism

This is the biggest difference. Peloton uses a magnetic resistance system with a felt pad that presses against the flywheel. It feels smooth, but it is not true electromagnetic resistance. The resistance levels are arbitrary numbers from 1-100. You can't calibrate them to real-world watts.

The NordicTrack S22i uses a 32-pound inertia-enhanced flywheel with automatic digital resistance. It can adjust resistance in real-time based on the terrain of the video you are watching. This is huge for training. If you are doing a virtual ride up a mountain, the bike makes it harder automatically. Peloton makes you manually turn the knob.

2. Metric Accuracy and Connectivity

If you train with power (watts), this matters more than anything. The Peloton Bike+ has a built-in power meter that is accurate to within 2-3% of a professional power meter. The base Peloton Bike does not have a power meter; it only estimates output via a formula that is often inflated by 20-30% compared to real-world power.

The NordicTrack S22i also lacks a true power meter. It shows a "watts" number, but it is calculated from speed and resistance, not measured from strain gauges. For serious training, you will want to use your own power meter pedals (like Garmin or Assioma) with either bike, but the Peloton Bike+ is the only one that natively integrates with them for accurate calibration.

3. The Ecosystem and Coaching

Peloton's library is the gold standard. Thousands of classes, from 5-minute warm-ups to 90-minute endurance rides. The instructors are top-tier, and the leaderboard adds a competitive edge that keeps you honest. But the membership is $44/month for the All-Access plan.

NordicTrack uses iFit. It costs $39/month (or you can buy a 1-year family plan for $396). iFit's strength is its "Google Maps" technology. You can ride real-world routes, and the bike automatically adjusts the incline and resistance to match the terrain. The downside is that the video quality and instructor energy are not as polished as Peloton.

4. Build Quality and Comfort

Both bikes are heavy and stable. The Peloton Bike weighs 135 lbs and has a 4-way adjustable seat and handlebars. The NordicTrack S22i weighs 205 lbs and has the same adjustments but adds a 22-inch pivoting touchscreen that makes it easier to do off-bike strength training.

The S22i also has a unique feature: it tilts up to 20% incline and -10% decline. This simulates real hills better than any stationary bike. It is not just a gimmick. It engages your glutes and hamstrings differently on climbs. But it also adds mechanical complexity and potential failure points.

Product Reviews

Peloton Bike (Original)

Price: $1,445 (bike only). Rating: 4.5/5

This is the value king of the Peloton lineup. It has the same frame, flywheel, and 21.5-inch HD touchscreen as the more expensive Bike+. The only major loss is the lack of a built-in power meter and the auto-follow resistance feature.

For serious training, the lack of a power meter is annoying, but not a dealbreaker. You can buy a set of power meter pedals for around $400 and use them with the Peloton Bike. The bike will read the power from your pedals and display it on the screen. This gives you accurate data without spending $1,000 more on the Bike+.

The resistance is manual, which is actually a pro for many serious cyclists. You control the load precisely. You are not fighting an automatic system that reacts to a video. The 1080p screen is crisp, and the speakers are surprisingly loud and clear.

Pros: Cheapest entry point to the Peloton ecosystem, accurate when paired with external power meter, massive class library, sturdy build.

Cons: No incline feature, manual resistance knob feels dated, screen does not swivel for floor workouts.

Peloton Bike+

Price: $2,995. Rating: 5/5

This is the bike for the data-obsessed athlete. The Bike+ has a built-in power meter that is accurate to within 2-3% of a professional unit. This is critical if you are doing structured workouts like FTP tests, interval training, or following a training plan that specifies exact wattage targets.

The 23.8-inch rotating screen is a major upgrade. It swivels 90 degrees, so you can use it for the excellent Peloton strength, yoga, and stretching classes without contorting your neck. The auto-follow resistance feature is not a gimmick. When your instructor calls out a range (e.g., "45-50 resistance"), the bike automatically adjusts to the target. You can override it with the knob anytime. This feature is incredibly useful for high-intensity interval training (HIIT) where you are switching between hard efforts and recovery.

The sound system is also upgraded with a 2-channel speaker that sounds better than most Bluetooth speakers.

Pros: Accurate power meter, auto-follow resistance, rotating screen, best-in-class ecosystem.

Cons: Expensive, no incline/decline feature, membership is another $44/month.

NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle

Price: $1,999-$2,999 (frequent sales include a 1-year iFit membership). Rating: 4/5

The S22i is the most feature-packed bike under $3,000. The 22-inch rotating touchscreen is huge and crisp. The 32-pound flywheel is heavy and smooth. But the standout feature is the automatic incline and decline. The bike tilts up to 20% and down to -10%. This is not a gimmick. When you ride a virtual route in the French Alps, the bike physically pushes you up a hill. Your quads and glutes feel the difference.

The iFit platform is the other differentiator. The "Google Maps" rides sync with the bike's resistance and incline automatically. You can ride the actual Tour de France route from your living room, and the bike reacts to every gradient change. This is the most immersive training experience you can buy.

However, the metrics are the weak point. The S22i does not have a true power meter. The "watts" it displays are an estimate that I found to be wildly inaccurate. On a steady-state effort, my Garmin power pedals showed 220 watts while the S22i displayed 280 watts. That is a 27% difference. You cannot train with power on this bike without buying external pedals, and even then, the bike's screen will not display the pedal data natively.

The iFit classes are good but not Peloton-good. The instructors are less charismatic, and the video production quality is lower. The leaderboard feature is also less compelling, as it is based on iFit's inflated output numbers.

Pros: Incline/decline feature is unmatched, immersive Google Maps rides, huge rotating screen, cheaper membership.

Cons: Inaccurate power estimates, video quality is lower than Peloton, mechanical complexity may lead to more maintenance.

Schwinn IC4 (The Budget Alternative)

Price: $999. Rating: 4/5

If you are serious about training but do not want to pay for a screen subscription, the Schwinn IC4 is the smart buy. It has a 40-pound flywheel, magnetic resistance, and a built-in cadence sensor. It does not have a screen, but it has a tablet holder and Bluetooth connectivity that works with apps like Zwift, Peloton (the app, not the bike), and Kinetic.

The IC4 broadcasts power, speed, and cadence via Bluetooth and ANT+. The power data is estimated, but it is much more accurate than the NordicTrack. In my testing, it was within 5% of my power meter pedals. This makes it a legitimate training tool for less than half the price of the Peloton Bike+.

Pros: Cheap, accurate enough for most training, works with any app, no subscription required.

Cons: No built-in screen, no incline feature, the seat and handlebar adjustments are less precise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use the Peloton app on a NordicTrack S22i?

Technically, yes. You can run the Peloton app on the S22i's Android-based tablet. However, you will lose the auto-resistance and incline features, as those are locked to the iFit app. You will just be manually changing resistance on a bike you paid extra for. It is a waste of the hardware.

Which bike has better classes?

Peloton wins for fitness classes. The instructors are better coaches, the music is better, and the community is more engaging. iFit wins for scenic rides. If you love exploring real-world routes, iFit is more fun.

Are the resistance levels the same between bikes?

No. Peloton uses a 1-100 scale. NordicTrack uses a 1-24 scale. You cannot directly compare the numbers. A "50" on a Peloton is roughly a "13" on a NordicTrack, but it depends on the flywheel speed. This is why a power meter is essential for serious training.

Is the NordicTrack incline feature worth it?

It depends. If you live in a flat area and want to train for hilly races, yes. The incline genuinely works your posterior chain. If you are mostly doing indoor interval training, the incline is a gimmick you will rarely use.

What is the warranty like?

Peloton offers a 12-month warranty on the bike frame and parts, with an optional 39-month extended warranty for $349. NordicTrack offers a 10-year frame, 2-year parts, and 1-year labor warranty. iFit is better for long-term peace of mind.

Bottom Line Verdict

Buy the Peloton Bike+ if you are a data-driven cyclist who wants accurate power numbers, a huge class library, and the best coaching in the industry. The auto-follow resistance and rotating screen justify the $2,995 price tag. Save money with the base Peloton Bike if you are willing to buy external power meter pedals and do not need the screen to swivel. Buy the NordicTrack S22i only if you value the incline feature and immersive Google Maps rides over metric accuracy. The inflated power numbers will frustrate you if you are training to a specific wattage. The Schwinn IC4 is the smart money pick if you want to use Zwift or the Peloton app without the hardware markup. For my money, the Peloton Bike+ is the only one that feels like a serious training tool, not just a fancy exercise bike.
