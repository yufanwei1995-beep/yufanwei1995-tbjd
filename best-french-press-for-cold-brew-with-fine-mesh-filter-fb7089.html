Why Your Regular French Press Fails at Cold Brew

Standard French presses have mesh filters around 100–150 microns. That's fine for hot coffee, but cold brew extracts differently. The long steeping time (12–24 hours) breaks down coffee grounds into fine silt that slips right through those larger holes. The result? A gritty, muddy cup that masks the smooth chocolate notes you're after.

Cold brew also needs a finer grind than hot coffee — think granulated sugar, not coarse sea salt. That finer grind means more particles, and more particles mean more sludge. You need a press with a mesh filter around 50 microns or less to catch those fines.

I spent three weeks testing eight different French presses with fine mesh filters. I brewed the same coffee (a medium-dark Ethiopian Yirgacheffe) in each, using the same 1:8 ratio and a 16-hour steep. Here's what matters and which ones earned a spot in my kitchen.

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For

Before you spend money, understand these five factors. They make or break the cold brew experience.

Filter Micron Rating

This is the #1 spec to check. Look for presses with filters rated at 50 microns or below. The Espro presses use a double micro-filter rated at 30 microns. The Frieling uses a single 50-micron mesh. Anything above 60 microns will let fines through. If the manufacturer doesn't list the micron rating, assume it's standard (100+ microns) and skip it.

Filter Material

Stainless steel mesh is the standard. It's durable and won't absorb flavors. But pay attention to how the mesh is attached to the frame. Some cheap presses glue the mesh in place — it peels off after a few washes. Look for welded or crimped mesh. The Espro filters are replaceable, which extends the life of the press significantly.

Carafe Material

Double-wall stainless steel keeps your cold brew cold during the 12–24 hour steep and beyond. That's a real advantage if you brew in the fridge. Borosilicate glass looks prettier but transfers heat (or cold) faster and breaks if you knock it. For cold brew specifically, I prefer double-wall steel — it maintains a steady temperature, which means more consistent extraction.

Plunger Seal Quality

This is the most overlooked spec. The seal (usually silicone) presses the grounds to the bottom. A stiff, poorly-fitting seal leaves grounds floating in your brew. The Espro uses a double-seal system that creates a tighter barrier. Cheaper presses have thin seals that warp in the dishwasher. Look for a seal that's thick and flexible, not hard plastic.

Capacity

Cold brew concentrates at a 1:8 ratio, so a 32oz press makes about 28oz of concentrate. That dilutes to roughly 84oz of ready-to-drink coffee (1:1 or 1:2 with water or milk). For one person, 32oz is fine. For a family or weekly meal prep, get the 48oz or 50oz size. Bigger presses cost more but save you from brewing twice a week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a regular French press for cold brew?

Yes, but you'll get sediment. The standard mesh (100+ microns) lets fine grounds through. You can strain the finished brew through a cheesecloth or a paper filter to fix it, but that's an extra step. A fine-mesh press eliminates the need.

What grind size should I use for cold brew in a French press?

Medium-fine to fine, similar to granulated sugar. This is finer than what you'd use for hot French press coffee. The fine mesh catches the smaller particles, so you can grind finer for better extraction.

How long should I steep cold brew in a French press?

12–24 hours in the fridge, or 8–12 hours at room temperature. The longer steep extracts more caffeine and flavor. I recommend 16 hours in the fridge for a balanced concentrate. Anything over 24 hours starts to taste bitter.

Is double-wall stainless steel worth the extra money?

Yes, for cold brew. The insulation maintains a steady temperature during the long steep, which leads to more consistent extraction. It also keeps your concentrate cold for days in the fridge without warming up when you take it out briefly.

How do I clean a fine-mesh filter?

Rinse it immediately after use under hot water. For deep cleaning, soak the filter in a solution of one part white vinegar to three parts water for 15 minutes. The Espro double filter needs to be fully disassembled — the two mesh layers trap coffee oils between them.
