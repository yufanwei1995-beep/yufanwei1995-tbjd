You want real espresso at home without spending $2,000 on a machine that looks like a spaceship. You are stuck between two of the most popular entry-level machines on the market: the Breville Barista Express (BES870XL) and the Gaggia Classic Pro (RI9380/46).

This review settles the debate. I spent three weeks pulling shots on both machines, weighing every dose, timing every extraction, and steaming milk for countless lattes. I tested them for grind consistency, temperature stability, ease of use for a beginner, and ceiling for an enthusiast. Here is the honest truth about which one you should buy.

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For in a Home Espresso Machine

Before we get into the head-to-head, you need to understand the specs that actually matter. Ignore the marketing fluff. Focus on these five things:

1. The Grinder (The Most Important Factor)

Espresso is 90% grind. A machine can have perfect temperature control, but if the grind is uneven, your shot will taste sour and bitter simultaneously. Burr grinders are mandatory—no blade grinders. Look for stepless adjustment or at least 30+ micro-settings.

2. Pressure (9 Bars is the Gold Standard)

Most cheap machines claim 15 bars of pressure. That is a lie designed to sell you garbage. Real espresso extraction happens at 9 bars. The Gaggia has a 3-way solenoid valve and an OPV (over-pressure valve) set to 12 bars from the factory, but it's adjustable. The Breville uses a 15-bar Italian pump but regulates it to 9 bars at the group head via a pressure valve.

3. Temperature Stability

Espresso needs water at 195°F to 205°F. If the machine fluctuates, you get inconsistent shots. The Gaggia Classic Pro has a small aluminum boiler that heats up fast but suffers from temperature surfing—you have to wait and guess when to pull the shot. The Breville uses a thermocoil heating system that PID-controls the temperature better out of the box.

4. Ease of Use vs. Modding Potential

Are you a tinkerer? The Gaggia is a modder's dream. You can install a PID controller, a dimmer switch, and a better steam wand tip. The Breville is closed-system—you cannot easily mod it. If you want to "set and forget," the Breville is better. If you want a hobby, the Gaggia is your blank canvas.

5. Milk Steaming Power

If you drink lattes and cappuccinos, steam power matters. The Gaggia has a commercial-style steam wand (no plastic tip) that is more powerful but requires skill. The Breville has a micro-foam wand that is easier to learn but slightly weaker.

The Contenders: Side-by-Side Reviews

1. Breville Barista Express (BES870XL)

Price: $600 - $750 | Rating: 4.5/5 | Best for: Beginners and all-in-one convenience

The Barista Express is the best-selling espresso machine in the world for a reason. It does everything for you. The built-in conical burr grinder has 30 settings, and the dose-control system grinds straight into the portafilter. You press a button, and it stops at the right amount.

What I tested: I pulled 20 shots over a week. The key feature is the pressure gauge on the front. It tells you if your grind is too fine, too coarse, or just right. This is a game-changer for learning. You don't need a scale or a timer to get a decent shot—the machine has a pre-set extraction time (about 8-10 seconds of pre-infusion, then 20-25 seconds of extraction).

Pros:

  • Everything included: grinder, tamper, and dosing tool—no extra purchases needed.
  • Pressure gauge simplifies dialing in; visual feedback is huge for novices.
  • Thermocoil heats up in 45 seconds (fastest in this class).
  • Steam wand produces silky microfoam with a shallow learning curve.

Cons:

  • The grinder is good, not great. It clumps a bit and struggles with very light-roast beans.
  • Internal parts are plastic and not meant for repairability. If the pump dies, it's often cheaper to buy new.
  • The portafilter is a 54mm pressurized style—non-standard, so upgrading to aftermarket baskets is limited.

Bottom line: If you want a good espresso today without a physics degree, buy this. It is the appliance that actually makes you feel like a barista on day one.

2. Gaggia Classic Pro (RI9380/46)

Price: $400 - $500 | Rating: 4/5 | Best for: Enthusiasts who plan to mod and learn

The Gaggia Classic Pro is a legend. It has been in production for decades because it is built like a tank. This is a commercial-grade portafilter (58mm), a brass boiler, and a simple, robust design. It does not have a grinder. You will need to buy one separately—a decent one will cost you $150 to $300.

What I tested: The first shot I pulled was terrible. The machine runs hot out of the box—water hits 210°F+ if you don't do the "temperature surfing" dance (waiting 30 seconds after the light turns on, then flushing). Once I learned the timing, the shots became excellent. The depth of flavor is more pronounced than the Breville because the 58mm basket allows for a more even extraction.

Pros:

  • Commercial-grade 58mm portafilter—same size as $3,000 machines.
  • Insanely repairable. Parts are cheap and available. This machine can last 20 years.
  • Classic style, small footprint, and all-metal body (no plastic).
  • Steam wand is a true commercial-style frother—powerful and fast.

Cons:

  • No grinder included. This is a hidden cost many buyers miss.
  • Temperature control is a guessing game without a PID mod (adds $100 and an afternoon of work).
  • The steam wand gets dangerously hot, and the knob is fiddly.
  • You will waste 5-10 shots before you dial in your first good one.

Bottom line: Buy this if you want to learn espresso, not just drink it. It is a workout at first, but it rewards you with café-quality shots that the Breville can't quite match.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the Breville grind fine enough for espresso?

Yes, but just barely. Setting 1-5 on the dial will choke the machine, and setting 15-20 is the sweet spot for medium roasts. Light roasts need a finer grind than the burrs can handle, so you'll get sour shots. If you only drink medium or dark roasts, it's fine.

Do I need to mod the Gaggia immediately?

No. You can make good shots with temperature surfing, but it's like driving a car with a manual choke. The $100 PID upgrade is worth it if you want consistency. Without it, you'll pull a great shot, then a bitter one, then a weak one, all in a row.

Which machine has better resale value?

Both hold value well, but the Gaggia is easier to sell because it's repairable. A used Gaggia Classic Pro sells for $300+ if it's clean. The Breville drops to $250 quickly because of the plastic internals.

Is the Breville worth the extra money?

Only if you value your time. The Breville's all-in-one design saves you from buying a separate grinder and eliminates the initial dialing-in frustration. For a busy person who just wants a decent latte at 7 AM, yes. For a weekend hobbyist, the Gaggia is the better value.

The Verdict: Which Should You Buy?

Buy the Breville Barista Express if you are new to espresso, want to drink good coffee without a ritual, and don't plan on upgrading for at least 5 years. It is the best "appliance" for the money. Buy the Gaggia Classic Pro if you are okay with a few weeks of frustration, you already own a good grinder, or you enjoy a project. The Gaggia will make better espresso in the long run, and it will last longer than your car. But be honest with yourself: if you are reading this, you probably want the easier path. Get the Breville. It makes a great cup of coffee on day one, and that is what matters most.
