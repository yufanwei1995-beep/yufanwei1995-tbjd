What to Look For in a Rainy-Climate Compost Bin

Not all bins are created equal. Here's the checklist I use after testing 20+ bins over the last decade.

1. The Lid Lock is the Whole Game

Cheap bins use a friction-fit lid. Rain swells the plastic, wind catches the edge, and it's gone. You need a mechanical lock — either a cam-lock, a twist-lock, or a bungee-style latch. The FCMP IM4000 has a twist-lock that requires a quarter turn. The Envirocycle uses a bolt-through handle. If a bin just says "snaps shut," it's not locked.

2. Drainage is Not Optional

In a rainy climate, your bin will fill with water. If it doesn't have drainage holes in the base, you're making anaerobic sludge, not compost. Look for a floor that's raised with holes, or a system that lets leachate escape. The Geobin is basically a wire mesh cage — it drains perfectly but dries out fast. The FCMP has a solid base with small weep holes.

3. Wall Thickness and UV Stabilizers

Thin plastic (< 1/8 inch) will crack in a freeze-thaw cycle. You want high-density polyethylene (HDPE) or polypropylene. Look for "UV stabilized" in the specs — that means it won't turn brittle after a season of sun. The Envirocycle is made from 100% recycled resin and has held up for 4 years in my Pacific Northwest yard.

4. Tumbler vs. Stationary

In wet climates, tumblers win for speed because you can spin them to aerate after a rain. Stationary bins are cheaper but need manual turning with a fork. If you go stationary, get one with a wide opening at the bottom for easy removal of finished compost — digging out the bottom of a wet bin is miserable.

5. Critter Resistance

Rain brings worms, but rain also brings rats. A good lock keeps raccoons out. The lid needs to be flush with the body — no gaps. The Envirocycle has a gasket seal that I've watched a raccoon try to pry open for 10 minutes without success.

Pro tip: If you're in a windy area, buy a bin with a base that can be staked down. The FCMP has pre-drilled holes for ground stakes. A bin that tips over in a storm is a bin that spills your compost.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I keep compost from getting too wet in the rain?

You need drainage holes at the bottom and a lid that seals. If your bin fills with water, it's not draining. Add more browns (dried leaves, cardboard) to balance the moisture. In extreme rain, move the bin under a tarp or roof overhang.

Do I need a tumbler or a stationary bin?

In a rainy climate, a tumbler is better because you can spin it to aerate the pile after a downpour. Stationary bins get compacted and waterlogged. If you have physical limitations, a tumbler is also easier on your back — no fork turning.

Will a lid lock keep raccoons out?

Yes, if it's a mechanical lock that creates a tight seal. The FCMP and Envirocycle have gaskets that raccoons can't pry open. The Geobin's bungee cord will not stop a determined raccoon.

How long does compost take in a rainy climate?

With a tumbler, you can get finished compost in 4-6 weeks during the warm season. In winter rain, it slows down to 8-12 weeks because the pile stays cold and wet. The Jora's steel body helps retain heat, which speeds things up.

Can I leave my compost bin out in winter?

Yes, but look for UV-stabilized plastic or steel. Cheap plastic will crack in freeze-thaw cycles. The Envirocycle and Jora are the most winter-hardy options here.
