This review is for cyclists who don't care about scenic rides or scenic routes. You want to grind. You want resistance that feels like a 15% grade in the Alps, not a bump in a parking lot. I spent 6 weeks alternating between the standard Peloton Bike (not the Bike+) and the NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle, specifically hammering their hill climb programs. Here's what broke, what worked, and which one you should buy for steep hill training.

Quick Summary:
  • Who this is for: Road cyclists and spin junkies who need sustained, heavy resistance for hill climbing intervals, not just cadence work.
  • Top Pick: NordicTrack S22i for pure hill simulation. The 20% motorized incline and 10% decline feel real, but the Peloton wins on class quality and community.
  • Price Range: Peloton Bike ($1,445 with delivery) vs NordicTrack S22i ($1,999, often on sale for $1,499). Both require separate monthly subscriptions.

Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters for Hill Training

Before comparing these two, forget the touchscreens for a minute. Hill training is about torque and resistance curve. Here are the specs that matter:

  • Max Resistance (in kg or Ohms): Peloton uses a magnetic brake system rated with a "resistance range" of 0-100. The S22i uses a 32-pound inertia-enhanced flywheel with a manual resistance knob PLUS a motorized incline. The S22i's resistance is measured in "digital resistance levels" (0-24) on top of the incline.
  • Incline/Decline: The NordicTrack tilts the whole bike. That changes your hip angle and engages your glutes differently than just cranking resistance. Peloton has zero incline — it's a fixed frame that simulates hills only through magnetic drag.
  • Flywheel Weight: Heavier flywheels (S22i's 32 lbs) smooth out pedal strokes at low cadence. Lighter flywheels (Peloton's 30 lbs) require more constant effort but feel more responsive.
  • Content for Climbs: Peloton's class library has specific "Climb Rides" that choreograph resistance targets. NordicTrack has iFit, which auto-syncs the incline to real-world video trails. If you train for actual outdoor climbs, auto-incline is a game-changer.
Pro Tip: If you're a power meter nerd, neither bike has a true power meter. They estimate watts. The Peloton's estimate is notoriously generous (adds 10-20% to your real output). The S22i is more conservative. Don't compare your numbers across bikes.

Peloton Bike vs NordicTrack S22i: Deep Dive Reviews

1. Peloton Bike (Original, not Bike+)

The standard Peloton Bike has been around since 2014. It's a tank. The 21.5-inch touchscreen is old but snappy. For hill training, the key is the resistance knob — it's smooth, continuous, and precise. You can micro-adjust from 40 to 45 resistance with a quarter turn. That matters when you're dying on a 45-minute climb and need to shave off 5% effort without breaking form.

The "Climb Rides" are excellent. Instructors like Ben Alldis and Matt Wilpers structure these like actual outdoor climbs: warm-up, sustained threshold, then sprints out of the saddle. The downside? You're staring at a flat screen with a static bike. The resistance is all you get. It's mentally tougher, honestly.

Specs: 30 lb flywheel, 0-100 digital resistance, fixed frame, 21.5" HD touchscreen, Bluetooth for heart rate.

The Bad: The seat is a brick. You'll need a padded cover. The subscription is $44/month and pricey. Also, the bike is loud — that magnetic brake whirrs like a jet engine on high resistance.

2. NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle

This is the hill monster. The 20% incline and 10% decline are motorized. When iFit's trail video shows a steep gravel road, the bike literally tilts down in front, shifting your weight forward. It's disorienting at first, then addictive. For hill training, the incline forces you to stand up and grind in a way a flat frame can't replicate.

The S22i has a 32-pound flywheel, and the resistance knob has 24 digital levels. But here's the catch: the resistance levels are gated. You can't fine-tune between levels 14 and 15 — it's a click, not a smooth sweep. That's frustrating for endurance work. The incline, however, is continuous via a motor control, so you get smooth gradient changes.

Specs: 32 lb flywheel, 0-24 resistance levels, 20% incline/10% decline, 22" rotating touchscreen, auto-fan.

The Bad: Build quality is less premium than Peloton. The plastic pedal arms flex under heavy out-of-saddle efforts. The iFit subscription is $39/month, but the bike goes on sale for $1,499 regularly, making it cheaper upfront. The auto-incline is only as good as the wi-fi connection — if it drops, the bike freezes.

3. Peloton Bike+ (The Upgrade Worth Considering)

I'm including the Bike+ because if you're serious about hills, the auto-resistance feature is a godsend. The Bike+ has a motor that physically turns the resistance knob for you based on the instructor's targets. You still don't get incline, but the resistance is continuous (0-100) and the motor is silent. It also has a 24-inch screen and better speakers.

For hill training, the Bike+ is the Peloton to buy. The auto-resistance means you can't cheat — the bike forces you to hit the target. The downside? It's $2,995. That's $1,000 more than the S22i at full price.

The Bad: Price. Also, the auto-resistance is tied to the Peloton class, so if you're doing a "Power Zone" ride where the instructor says "RPE 7" instead of a number, the auto-resistance doesn't work.

4. Bowflex C6 (The Budget Alternative)

If you want hill training without subscriptions, the Bowflex C6 is a decent fallback. It has 100 micro-adjustable resistance levels (same magnetic system as Peloton) and a 40-pound flywheel — the heaviest here. It's compatible with the Peloton app, Zwift, and others via Bluetooth. But there's no incline, and the included cadence sensor is cheap.

For steep hill work, the heavy flywheel is excellent. It maintains momentum at low cadence, which is exactly what you need for grinding climbs. The JRNY app (included for a year) has some scenic rides, but it's not iFit or Peloton quality.

The Bad: The frame wobbles at high resistance. The handlebars are flexy. It's a budget bike, but it does the resistance job well.

FAQ

Can the Peloton simulate a 15% grade for hill training?

Sort of. The resistance knob maxes out at 100, which feels like a heavy gear on a trainer. But without incline, your body position stays the same. For glute activation and hip hinge, the S22i is superior. For pure leg torque, Peloton's resistance is harder than S22i's max.

Is the S22i's incline worth the subscription cost?

If you train for outdoor hills, yes. The auto-incline matches real trails in iFit. That means you're constantly adjusting your pedaling style — exactly what you need for race day. If you just want to sweat indoors, save the money and get the Peloton.

Which bike has better classes for hill intervals?

Peloton. Their "Climb Rides" are structured with specific resistance ranges and cadence targets. iFit is more of a "follow the trail" experience, which is less precise for structured intervals.

Bottom Line Verdict

Buy the NordicTrack S22i if your priority is replicating steep outdoor climbs. The 20% motorized incline changes your body mechanics in ways a flat bike can't, and the heavy flywheel handles slow-cadence grinding beautifully. But be ready to deal with clunky resistance steps and a less polished app. Buy the Peloton Bike (or Bike+) if you want the best coaching and community, and you're okay with simulating hills purely through magnetic drag — it's mentally harder, but the class structure will make you a stronger hill climber on a flat bike. For most riders, the S22i is the better hill trainer; the Peloton is the better all-around fitness investment.
