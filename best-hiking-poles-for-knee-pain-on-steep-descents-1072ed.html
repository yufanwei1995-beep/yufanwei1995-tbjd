If you've ever felt your kneecap scream on a 1,000-foot descent, you know the problem. Every step sends a shockwave up your leg. Your quads burn. Your knees ache for days after.

Trekking poles aren't just for old folks or ultralight nerds. For downhill sections, they're the single best tool you can carry. They transfer the impact from your knees to your arms. Done right, they can cut the force on your knees by up to 25%.

But not all poles are equal. I've spent the last three months testing five popular models on rocky descents, loose scree, and muddy trails. I measured shock absorption, grip comfort, and how well they held up after 50+ miles. Here's what I found.

What to Look For in Hiking Poles for Knee Pain

Before we get to the reviews, you need to understand the three things that actually matter for knee pain. Ignore the marketing fluff about "aerospace-grade aluminum" and focus on this.

1. Shock Absorption System

This is the #1 feature for knee pain. A built-in spring or dampening system in the shaft absorbs the impact so your arm—not your knee—takes the beating. Look for poles with an internal coil spring (like Black Diamond's FlickLock) or an external spring (like Leki's Aergon).

Without shock absorption, a stiff pole just transfers the jolt from the ground to your wrist and shoulder. That's not helping anyone.

2. Grip Material and Shape

Cork is king. It molds to your hand, wicks sweat, and doesn't get slippery when wet. Foam is second best. Rubber is heavy and makes your hands sweat like crazy in summer.

Also check the grip shape. Ergonomic, angled grips (like Leki's Aergon or Black Diamond's Trail Ergo) let your wrist stay neutral. That reduces strain on your forearms during long descents.

3. Weight and Packed Length

Lighter poles mean less arm fatigue. But there's a tradeoff: ultra-light poles (under 16 oz per pair) are usually less durable and have weaker locking mechanisms. For knee pain, you want a pole that's sturdy enough to take your full body weight on a steep step down.

Packed length matters if you plan to strap them to a backpack. Three-section poles pack down to about 24 inches. Two-section poles are stiffer but harder to store.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do hiking poles actually help with knee pain?

Yes, if you use them correctly. The key is to plant the pole and push down with your arms as you step down. This shifts the load from your knees to your upper body. Studies show this can reduce the compressive force on your knee joints by up to 25% on descents. But only if you're actually using them — not just carrying them.

How do I use poles on a steep descent?

Shorten your poles by 2-3 inches before you start going down. Plant both poles slightly ahead of your feet, then lower yourself using your arms. Keep your back straight and your core engaged. Don't lean back on the poles — that puts pressure on your wrists.

Are carbon fiber poles worth the extra money?

For knee pain, yes. Carbon absorbs more vibration than aluminum, which means less impact transmitted to your hands and arms. It's also lighter, so your arms don't fatigue as fast. The downside is durability — carbon can snap if you stress it sideways. Aluminum bends, carbon breaks.

Do I need shock-absorbing poles?

If knee pain is your main concern, yes. A pole with a built-in spring absorbs the impact of each step before it reaches your wrist. On steep descents, this makes a noticeable difference. The Cascade Mountain Tech poles are a great value, but the lack of shock absorption was the single biggest difference I noticed between them and the pricier models.

Bottom Line

It has the most effective shock absorption system, a comfortable cork grip, and a rock-solid locking mechanism.
