Who This Is For and What Problem This Solves

You're planning a 4-7 day backpacking trip. You need to keep your phone, headlamp, and maybe a GPS device charged. You're standing in the aisle looking at solar panels and big battery bricks, and you have no idea which one won't leave you stranded. This guide breaks down the real tradeoffs between solar power banks and standard battery packs so you can stop guessing and start hiking.

Quick Summary
  • Who this is for: Backpackers on trips longer than 2 nights who need to charge small devices (phones, headlamps, wearables).
  • Our top pick: Anker PowerCore 20000 for pure battery reliability, or the BioLite SolarPanel 10+ for extended sun-exposed trips.
  • Price range: $40 to $180 depending on capacity and solar integration.

Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters on the Trail

Before you buy anything, understand these five factors. They decide everything.

1. Weight Per Watt-Hour

This is the math that matters. A standard 10,000mAh battery pack weighs around 7-8 ounces. A 20,000mAh pack is 12-14 ounces. Solar panels add 10-16 ounces on their own, plus you still need a battery to store that energy. On a multi-day trip, every ounce hurts. Look up the watt-hour (Wh) rating, not just mAh. It's the real measure of stored energy.

2. Charging Speed and Efficiency

Solar panels are slow. Real-world output is usually 30-50% of the rated wattage. A 21W panel might deliver 8-10W in full sun. That's roughly 5-6 hours to charge a phone. Battery packs charge a phone in 1-2 hours flat. If you're moving at dawn and dusk, solar gets almost nothing. You stop for lunch, you might get 20-30 minutes of good sun.

3. Weather and Sun Exposure

Solar needs direct, unobstructed sunlight. Tree cover, overcast skies, and even dusty panels kill output. In the Pacific Northwest or the Smokies, solar is borderline useless. In the desert Southwest or the Sierras above the treeline, it's a different story. Be honest about where you hike.

4. Durability and Waterproofing

Battery packs are usually IPX5 or IP67 rated. That means they handle rain and splashes. Solar panels are less protected. The folding panels have exposed cells and fragile hinges. If you drop one on a rock, you might crack a cell and lose 20% of your output. Battery packs in a dry bag survive almost anything.

5. Rechargeability on the Trail

This is the big one. A battery pack has finite energy. Once it's dead, it's dead until you hit a town. Solar can theoretically recharge indefinitely, but only if you have sun and time. For trips longer than 5 days without resupply, solar starts to make sense. For shorter trips, a bigger battery is lighter and more reliable.

The Products: What I Actually Tested

Anker PowerCore 20000 PD

The gold standard for pure battery packs. This thing holds 20,000mAh (72Wh) and weighs 12.7 ounces. It charges an iPhone 15 Pro about five times from dead. The USB-C Power Delivery port pushes out 20W, which is enough to fast-charge most modern phones. There's also a standard USB-A port for older devices or a headlamp.

Build quality is typical Anker — solid, matte plastic that doesn't feel cheap. The LED indicator lights are basic but clear. It's not the lightest per Wh on the market, but it's reliable and doesn't get hot even when pushing 20W continuously.

The tradeoff: When this dies, you're done. No way to recharge it on the trail. For a 5-day trip where you're using your phone for navigation and photos, you'll likely have power left. For 7+ days, you might run dry.

BioLite SolarPanel 10+

This is a 10W folding solar panel that weighs 15.8 ounces. It's not the lightest, but it has a built-in 3,000mAh battery that charges while the panel is in the sun. You can charge your phone directly from the panel or use the internal battery as a buffer.

In full sun, I got about 6W of actual output — enough to charge a phone in 3-4 hours. The fabric exterior is ripstop nylon, and the built-in kickstand angles the panel toward the sun. It's well-built and survived a week of abuse in my pack.

The tradeoff: The internal battery is small, and the panel output is mediocre. You're carrying 16 ounces for what amounts to a slow trickle of power. This works if you're hiking in exposed terrain and stopping for long breaks. It's a poor choice for forested trails.

Goal Zero Nomad 10

Goal Zero has been in this game for a decade. The Nomad 10 is a 10W panel that weighs 13 ounces and folds down to the size of a thick book. It has a USB-A output and a kickstand. I tested this alongside the BioLite, and it performed similarly — about 5-7W in full sun.

The build is simpler than the BioLite. No internal battery, so you need to pair it with a separate power bank. That's actually a plus if you already own a battery pack. It also has a daisy-chain port to connect multiple panels, which is rare at this price point.

The tradeoff: No internal battery means no "trickle charge while you walk" strategy. You need to stop and plug your devices in. The plastic hinge feels less durable than the BioLite's fabric design.

Jackery Bolt 10000

This is a hybrid — a 10,000mAh battery pack with a built-in wall plug and USB-C cable. It weighs 8.1 ounces and charges a phone about 2.5 times. The unique feature is the fold-out wall prongs, so you can recharge it at any outlet without carrying a separate cable.

It's not solar, but it's the lightweight choice for trips under 4 days. The integrated USB-C cable is a nice touch — one less thing to lose. It charges at 18W, which is fast enough for most phones.

The tradeoff: No solar capability at all. The wall prongs add bulk, and 10,000mAh might not be enough for a full week of heavy phone use.

BigBlue 28W Solar Charger

This is the heavy hitter. A 28W four-panel foldable that weighs 21 ounces. It's overkill for most backpackers, but if you're leading a group or doing a 10-day traverse, this thing actually works. In full sun, I measured 18-20W output — enough to charge a tablet or two phones simultaneously.

The panels are thicker than the BioLite and Goal Zero, which makes them more durable but bulkier. It has two USB-A ports and one USB-C PD port. The built-in smart chip regulates output to protect your devices.

The tradeoff: 21 ounces is a lot of pack weight. This is for basecamp or group trips, not ultralight solo hikes. It also takes up a lot of room in your pack.

FAQ

Can solar panels charge a phone directly?

Yes, but it's painfully slow. Most panels need 3-5 hours of direct sun to fully charge a modern smartphone. You're better off charging a battery pack during the day and using the battery at night.

How much battery capacity do I need for a 5-day trip?

A 10,000mAh pack covers a phone for 2-3 days of moderate use (navigation, photos, messages). A 20,000mAh pack covers 5-6 days. If you're using a GPS device or camera that needs daily charging, double your capacity estimate.

Is solar ever worth the weight?

Only if you're going 6+ days without resupply, or if you're in consistently sunny terrain above the treeline. For most weekend and 3-4 day trips, a bigger battery pack is lighter and more reliable.

What about power banks with built-in solar panels?

Avoid them. The integrated panels are tiny (1-2W) and take 10+ hours to charge the bank itself. They're a gimmick. The only effective solar setups are separate folding panels that you can angle toward the sun.

Bottom Line Verdict

For the vast majority of multi-day backpackers, a battery pack is the right call. The Anker PowerCore 20000 gives you the most reliable power per ounce and costs half as much as a decent solar setup. It's simple, durable, and you never have to worry about clouds or tree cover. Solar only wins if you're on a 6+ day trip in exposed, sunny terrain — and even then, you need a quality panel like the BioLite or BigBlue, not a cheap integrated-solar power bank. Buy the Anker, put the weight savings into extra food, and don't overthink it.
