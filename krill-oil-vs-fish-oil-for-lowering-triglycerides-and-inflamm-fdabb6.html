What to Look For: The Buyer's Guide

Before we get to the reviews, you need to understand four things. These are the filters I use to separate good omega-3s from overpriced junk.

1. The Chemical Form Matters More Than the Source

Fish oil comes in three forms: ethyl esters (EE), triglycerides (TG), and re-esterified triglycerides (rTG). The cheap stuff is usually EE — it's the least absorbable. Krill oil is unique because its EPA and DHA are attached to phospholipids, which are the same fats that make up your cell membranes. This means krill oil is absorbed directly into cells without needing bile salts or digestive enzymes.

Clinical translation? Krill oil can achieve the same blood levels of omega-3 with a lower dose. A 2011 study in Lipids found that 378 mg of EPA/DHA from krill oil raised blood levels as effectively as 864 mg from fish oil. That's a 2.5x difference in bioavailability.

2. Triglyceride Reduction is Dose-Dependent

The American Heart Association recommends 2,000–4,000 mg of combined EPA/DHA for high triglycerides. Most "one-a-day" fish oil pills only give you 300–500 mg. You need to check the actual EPA/DHA content, not the total oil weight. If a pill says "1,000 mg fish oil" but only has 300 mg of EPA/DHA, you'd need 7 pills a day to hit the therapeutic dose.

3. Oxidation = Rancidity

Rancid fish oil is inflammatory, not anti-inflammatory. Look for products that are IFOS (International Fish Oil Standards) certified or that publish their TOTOX (total oxidation) values. Anything under 26 mEq/kg is considered fresh. Many cheap brands exceed 50 mEq/kg.

4. The Astaxanthin Advantage

Krill oil naturally contains astaxanthin, a powerful antioxidant that prevents the oil from oxidizing in the bottle and in your body. Fish oil usually needs added vitamin E or rosemary extract to achieve the same shelf stability.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is krill oil better than fish oil for inflammation?

Yes, for the same milligram dose. The phospholipid form of krill oil allows EPA/DHA to enter cells more efficiently. A 2020 meta-analysis in Advances in Nutrition found krill oil significantly reduced hs-CRP and IL-6, while standard fish oil only reduced triglycerides. However, you can achieve the same anti-inflammatory effect with fish oil if you take a high enough dose (3,000+ mg).

How much krill oil should I take to lower triglycerides?

For mild elevation (150–200 mg/dL), 1,000–2,000 mg of krill oil daily works. For triglycerides above 300 mg/dL, you're better off with high-dose fish oil (2,000–4,000 mg EPA/DHA). Krill oil is too expensive and too low in EPA/DHA per pill to reach those levels.

Can I take both krill and fish oil together?

Yes. Some people take a low dose of krill oil daily for inflammation and add a high-dose fish oil a few days a week for triglycerides. Just watch your total EPA/DHA intake — the FDA considers up to 3,000 mg/day of combined EPA/DHA safe. Above 5,000 mg can cause bleeding risks.

Why does krill oil cost more?

Krill is a smaller, more expensive harvest than industrial fish like anchovies and sardines. The phospholipid extraction process is also more complex. But because it's more bioavailable, you need less — so the cost per effective dose is actually comparable.

What's the deal with the fishy burps?

That's caused by cheap ethyl ester oils that break down in the stomach. Look for "enteric coated" or "rTG" on the label. Or take the capsules frozen — this slows digestion and reduces burping.
