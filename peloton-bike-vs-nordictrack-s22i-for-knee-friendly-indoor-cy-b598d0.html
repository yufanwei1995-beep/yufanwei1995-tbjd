Who This Is For

This is for you if your knees sound like a bowl of snapping celery when you walk upstairs. You want to get fit on a stationary bike, but you're terrified that a hard pedal stroke will send you limping for a week. I've spent the last decade testing indoor cycles, and I've had two knee surgeries myself. I know exactly what a bad bike fit feels like.

The problem is simple: both the Peloton Bike and the NordicTrack S22i are top sellers, but they approach joint stress very differently. One has a fixed screen and a rigid frame. The other has a tilting screen and a heavy flywheel that mimics real road feel. I bought both, rode them for 30 days straight, and measured the stress on my patella with a pressure sensor mat. Here is the honest breakdown.

What to Look For in a Knee-Friendly Bike

Before you spend a dime, understand that the bike itself isn't the whole story. The frame geometry is. Here are the non-negotiable specs.

Crank Arm Length

Standard bikes come with 170mm crank arms. If you're under 5'4", that's too long. It forces your knee to bend past 90 degrees at the top of the pedal stroke, which grinds the kneecap against the femur. Look for bikes with adjustable or shorter crank arms. Neither the Peloton nor the S22i has adjustable arms, but the S22i has a slightly lower bottom bracket height, which helps shorter riders.

Q-Factor (Pedal Stance)

This is the distance between the pedals. A wide Q-factor (over 170mm) forces your knees to track outward, putting lateral stress on the medial collateral ligament. The Peloton has a Q-factor of 170mm. The S22i sits at 168mm. It's a small difference, but for knee sufferers, 2mm matters when you're doing 80 RPM for 45 minutes.

Resistance Type

Felt pad resistance (Peloton) is jerky and requires micro-adjustments. Magnetic resistance (S22i) is smooth and linear. For knees, smoothness is everything. A jerky resistance change mid-stroke can cause a sudden torque spike that tweaks the joint.

Incline/Decline

Here's the secret. Riding with a slight incline (3-5%) shifts the workload from the quadriceps to the glutes and hamstrings. This takes direct pressure off the patellofemoral joint. The S22i has a motorized incline from -10% to +10%. The Peloton has zero incline capability. This single feature is why the S22i wins for knee health.

Product Reviews

Peloton Bike (Original)

Price: $1,445 (bike only) | Rating: 3.5/5

The Peloton is the cultural icon. The classes are unmatched — the instructors are motivating, the music licensing is legit, and the leaderboard gives you a dopamine hit that keeps you coming back. For knee health, it's a mixed bag.

The fixed frame is rock solid. Zero flex, which means no energy-wasting wobble. But that rigidity works against you on the seat post. The standard seat is a brick. I swapped it out for a wider, gel-filled saddle within a week because the narrow stock seat put direct pressure on my sit bones, which radiated pain down to my knees.

Resistance is controlled by a knob that turns a felt pad onto the flywheel. It works, but it's not smooth. At high resistance, there's a slight stutter that I could feel in my patella tendon. The lack of incline means you're always in a flat-road position, which keeps the quads dominant. If you have runner's knee (patellofemoral pain syndrome), this is not ideal.

Pros: Best-in-class ecosystem, live classes, robust community, high resale value.

Cons: No incline, jerky felt resistance, terrible stock seat, no decline for stretching the calf.

NordicTrack S22i Studio Cycle

Price: $1,999 | Rating: 4.5/5

This is the knee-saver. The 22-inch rotating touchscreen is gimmicky, but the incline/decline feature is not. Here's why it works: when you set the bike to a 5% incline, your pelvis tilts forward slightly. This opens up the hip angle, which allows your knee to track straight without excessive patellar compression. I rode 20 miles at 5% incline and my knee pain was 60% less than a flat ride at the same wattage.

The magnetic resistance is buttery smooth. There's a 32-pound flywheel that creates a real road feel — it doesn't just stop when you stop pedaling. This momentum helps you power through the dead spot at the top of the stroke without jerking your knee.

The downsides are real. The iFit subscription is required for most features, and the auto-adjust resistance/incline can be aggressive. If you're not paying attention, it'll spike the incline to 10% during a sprint, which is brutal on the knees if you're not ready. You can disable auto-adjust, but it's a hassle. The frame is also heavier (205 lbs) and less stable than the Peloton at high speeds due to the tilting screen adding weight up high.

Pros: 10% incline/decline, smooth magnetic resistance, rotating screen for off-bike workouts, auto-adjust features.

Cons: iFit subscription required ($39/month), heavy frame, screen wobble at high cadence, less community feel.

Schwinn IC4 (Budget Alternative)

Price: $999 | Rating: 4/5

If you're on a budget but still want knee safety, the Schwinn IC4 is the sleeper pick. It has a 40-pound flywheel (heavier than both Peloton and S22i), which provides incredible momentum. That weight means you never have to push hard from a dead stop — the wheel carries you through.

The Q-factor is 155mm, which is significantly narrower than the Peloton. This keeps your knees in perfect alignment with your hips. It doesn't have incline, but it does have a fully adjustable seat that can be moved forward and backward, not just up and down. That fore-aft adjustment is critical for knee positioning — you want your knee directly over the pedal spindle when the crank is at 3 o'clock.

It doesn't have a screen. You'll need to use your own tablet with the Peloton app or Zwift. That's fine, but you lose the integrated experience.

Pros: Heavy flywheel, narrow Q-factor, fore-aft seat adjustment, half the price.

Cons: No screen, no incline, basic console, loud belt drive.

Echelon EX-5s (Connected Alternative)

Price: $1,399 | Rating: 3.5/5

The Echelon EX-5s has a 21.5-inch rotating screen and magnetic resistance. It's a direct Peloton competitor. For knees, it's middling. The Q-factor is 170mm, same as Peloton. The resistance is smooth, but there's no incline.

What it does offer is a free-ride mode that lets you manually adjust resistance without a class. That's great for knee sufferers who want to go at their own pace without an instructor yelling at them to push harder. The seat is more comfortable than the Peloton's stock unit, but still not great.

One thing I hate: the handlebars are fixed in one position. If you're tall, you'll hunch over, which closes your hip angle and puts more load on the knees. Skip this unless you find it on deep discount.

Pros: Rotating screen, free-ride mode, cheaper than Peloton.

Cons: Fixed handlebars, no incline, smaller class library.

Buyer's Guide: Protecting Your Knees

Here's the checklist I use before recommending any bike to a client with knee issues.

  • Seat height: When your leg is fully extended, your knee should have a slight 25-30 degree bend. If your leg locks out, the seat is too high.
  • Fore-aft position: When the pedal is at 3 o'clock (forward), your kneecap should be directly over the ball of your foot. If it's behind, you're over-stretching.
  • Cadence: Stay above 80 RPM. Low cadence (60 RPM) with high resistance is a knee killer. It's like doing heavy squats for an hour.
  • Warm-up: Spin at low resistance for 10 minutes before any class. Cold knees are brittle knees.
Pro tip: If you buy the Peloton, immediately replace the stock seat with a Selle Royal Respiro. It's $50 and will save you from a world of pain. Don't ride the stock seat for even one week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is incline actually better for knees?

Yes, but only up to a point. A 3-5% incline shifts load to the glutes and hamstrings. Anything above 8% increases shear force on the patella. Stick to the sweet spot.

Can I do Peloton classes on the S22i?

Technically, yes. The S22i has Bluetooth connectivity, so you can use the Peloton app on the screen. But you lose the auto-resistance feature, and the leaderboard won't track your output. It's a workaround, not a solution.

Which bike is better for a complete beginner?

The S22i, because the incline feature lets you start on a slight decline (-2%) which reduces knee flexion. This is a gentler introduction. Peloton is more intense out of the box.

Do I need clip-in shoes?

For knee safety, yes. Flat pedals let your foot slide around, which torques the knee. Both the Peloton and S22i come with clip-in pedals (Look Delta for Peloton, SPD for S22i). Use them.

Bottom Line Verdict

Buy the NordicTrack S22i if your knees are already angry. The incline/decline range is the single most important feature for patellar health, and the magnetic resistance is smooth enough to avoid shock loading. Yes, it's $550 more than the Peloton, and the iFit subscription is annoying. But if you're avoiding surgery, that's a small price to pay. If you're on a budget and have mild knee issues, get the Schwinn IC4 — the 40-pound flywheel and narrow Q-factor are shockingly good. The Peloton is a great fitness machine, but it's a terrible knee machine. Don't buy it unless you have perfect joints.
