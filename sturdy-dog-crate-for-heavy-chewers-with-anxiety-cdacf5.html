Buyer's Guide: What to Look For in a Heavy-Duty Crate

Before you drop serious cash, understand what separates a "heavy duty" crate from a regular wire crate with a marketing sticker. Here are the four things that matter.

1. Gauge and Material of Steel

Most wire crates use 11-gauge or thinner steel. That’s toothpick territory. You need 7-gauge or thicker steel for a true chewer. Look for welded wire, not crimped. Crimped joints pop open. Welded is a solid piece. Some crates use expanded metal (like the Impact), which is a solid sheet of steel with diamond holes. That’s the strongest option.

2. Latching Mechanism

A standard slide-bolt latch is a toy for a smart dog. They learn to lift it in minutes. Look for double-locking, recessed, or bolt-action latches that are flush with the door. If the latch is exposed, a dog can grip it with their teeth. The best crates use a locking pin that requires a thumb to depress before sliding—physically impossible for a paw or a mouth to operate.

3. The Pan and Base

Do not overlook the floor pan. Plastic pans are a snack for a chewer. They will eat the tray, and you will be at the vet. Look for welded steel pans or reinforced aluminum. The pan needs to be bolted or flush to the frame. If it slides out from the inside, the dog will get under it.

4. Assembly and Portability

Here is the tradeoff. The crates that survive an anxious chewer weigh between 80 and 150 pounds. They are furniture. You will not be moving them around the house daily. If you need portability for travel, you are sacrificing safety. Decide if this is a stationary den (go heavy) or a travel crate (you will need a different solution, and your dog may still eat through it).

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a heavy chewer really destroy a metal crate?

Yes. A 70-pound dog can exert over 300 PSI of bite force. Standard 11-gauge wire bends like a paperclip. Even 7-gauge can be bent if the dog is determined and has enough time. That is why the Impact crate uses expanded steel—it has no flat surface to bite.

What is the best crate for separation anxiety?

The Richell wood crate is the best for anxiety because it creates a "den" environment. The solid walls block visual stimuli, which lowers cortisol levels. Metal crates are more open and can make anxious dogs feel exposed. However, if your dog panics and chews, you need the Impact.

Should I cover the crate with a blanket?

Yes, for most dogs. It helps with anxiety. But do not cover a wire crate if your dog is a chewer—they will pull the blanket through the bars and eat it, causing a blockage. Use a covered crate (like the Richell) or a metal crate with a fitted, chew-proof cover.

How do I stop my dog from chewing the crate?

First, rule out medical issues. Then, address the anxiety. Crate training is a process, not a punishment. Give them a frozen Kong or a puzzle toy to keep their mind occupied. And never let them out of the crate when they are barking or whining—that reinforces the behavior.

Bottom Line Verdict

If your dog is a true escape artist, a power chewer, or has severe panic attacks, buy the Impact High Anxiety crate. It costs over a thousand dollars, but it is the only crate on this list that guarantees your dog's safety and your sanity. If you can't justify that price, the ProSelect Empire is the best budget compromise—it will hold up to 90% of chewers, but you need to replace the plastic pan with a metal one immediately. For dogs that are anxious but not destructive, the Richell wood crate is the humane choice because it reduces stress instead of just containing it. Skip the MidWest if your dog has any history of bending metal. You will waste your money and risk your dog's teeth.
