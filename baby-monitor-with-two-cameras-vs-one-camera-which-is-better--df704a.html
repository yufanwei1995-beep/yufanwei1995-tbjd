Twins change everything. Double the feedings, double the diaper changes, and double the worry about whether both babies are breathing, sleeping, or about to roll over. A single-camera monitor forces you to choose which crib to watch. That's a terrible choice to make at 2 AM.

So, two cameras or one? The short answer: two cameras, almost always. But not all dual-camera setups are created equal. Some monitors let you add a second camera cheaply. Others force you into a whole new system. Here's what I learned after testing five different setups over three weeks with my neighbor's twin boys (with their permission, obviously).

What to Look for in a Twin Baby Monitor

Before we get into specific models, you need to understand what actually matters when you're monitoring two infants at once. This isn't the same as a single-baby setup.

Split-Screen vs. Cycling Views

The biggest difference between a good and bad twin monitor is how it displays both cameras. Split-screen shows both babies simultaneously. That's the gold standard. Cycling views (where the monitor switches between cameras every 10-15 seconds) means one baby is invisible for part of the time. That's not acceptable for twins.

Some monitors also offer picture-in-picture (PiP), where one camera is full-screen and the other is a smaller inset. That works fine, but split-screen is usually easier to read at a glance.

Camera Add-On Cost

Here's the trap. Some brands sell a single-camera monitor for $120, then charge $90 for an additional camera. Others sell a twin bundle for $250 that includes both cameras. You need to compare the total system price, not just the starter kit price.

Check whether the second camera connects to the same parent unit or requires a separate receiver. If it needs a separate receiver, you're juggling two screens. That defeats the purpose.

Range and Interference

Two cameras broadcasting at once doubles the chances of Wi-Fi interference or signal dropouts. Look for monitors that use FHSS (Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum) technology like the Infant Optics line. It's more stable than plain 2.4GHz Wi-Fi when you have multiple cameras.

For Wi-Fi monitors, make sure your router can handle two video streams simultaneously. Most modern routers can, but older ones might struggle, especially if you're also streaming Netflix.

Battery Life on the Parent Unit

With twins, you're not just checking in for a quick glance. You're watching both babies for extended periods. A parent unit that dies after 5 hours is useless. Look for at least 8-10 hours of continuous use. Also check if the parent unit has a battery-saving mode that turns off the screen but keeps audio on.

Night Vision Quality

You'll be using this monitor mostly in the dark. Test the night vision on both cameras. Some budget monitors have grainy, unusable night vision. Others, like the VTech and Infant Optics, have surprisingly clear infrared. Make sure the IR LEDs don't create hot spots or wash out the image.

I tested these monitors with two cribs spaced about 8 feet apart in the same room. That's the most common twin setup. I also tried them in separate rooms to test range and signal strength.

Rating: 4.5/5

The DXR-8 PRO is my top pick for twins. The base system comes with one camera, but you can buy the DXR-8 PRO camera separately and pair it with the same parent unit. The parent unit displays both cameras in split-screen mode with zero lag.

The image quality is excellent for a non-Wi-Fi monitor. The 5-inch screen is sharp, and the night vision is some of the best I've seen—both babies were clearly visible even in a pitch-black room. 2. VTech VM901-2 (Twin Pack)

Price: $180-$200 for the twin pack

Rating: 4/5

VTech sells a dedicated twin pack that includes two cameras and one parent unit right out of the box. No need to buy add-ons. The parent unit has a 5-inch screen that splits evenly between both cameras.

The standout feature here is the battery life. The parent unit lasts 10 hours on a full charge, which is the longest of any monitor I tested. The night vision is decent—slightly less sharp than the Infant Optics, but perfectly usable.

The downside: the cameras are fixed. You can't pan or tilt them remotely. You have to physically move the camera to adjust the view. That's annoying if your baby shifts position in the crib. Also, the range is a bit shorter than the Infant Optics—about 100 feet through walls.

One quirk: the split-screen puts both babies on one screen, but you can't enlarge one to full-screen without losing the other. It's all or nothing.

Bottom line: The best value for a true twin bundle. Set it up and forget it.

The Nanit is a Wi-Fi monitor, which means you watch the feed from your phone or tablet. That's a plus if you want to check on the babies from another room or while you're cooking dinner. It also has breathing tracking, which alerts you if your baby stops moving.

For twins, the Nanit app lets you view both cameras side-by-side on your phone. The image quality is stunning—1080p video with excellent night vision. The breathing tracking works on both babies simultaneously, which is a huge peace-of-mind feature.

Here's the catch: the Nanit requires a subscription for full features. The basic breathing tracking is free, but video history and smart alerts cost $50/year. And if your Wi-Fi goes down, you lose the feed entirely. That's a dealbreaker for some parents.

Also, the Nanit uses a floor stand that takes up space in the room. The camera needs to be mounted high, which makes it tricky to position over two cribs unless you have a good vantage point.

Bottom line: Best for tech-savvy parents who want phone viewing and breathing monitoring. Be prepared for the subscription cost.

Eufy's SpaceView Pro offers a 5.5-inch screen and a two-camera bundle at a reasonable price. The screen is the largest of any monitor I tested, which is nice when you're looking at two babies at once.

The biggest issue: the parent unit only cycles between the two cameras automatically. You have to manually switch to split-screen mode, and the transition is clunky. There's also a slight delay when switching, which is annoying.

Bottom line: A decent budget option, but the night vision issues and clunky switching make it hard to recommend over the VTech.

Bottom line: If budget is your only constraint, this works. But you get what you pay for in reliability.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a single camera to watch both twins?

Only if both cribs are side-by-side and the camera has a wide enough field of view. I tested this with the Infant Optics—you can see both babies, but you lose the ability to zoom in on one without losing the other. For newborns, that's fine. Once they start rolling or moving, you'll want individual cameras.

Do I need a Wi-Fi monitor for twins?

No. A dedicated monitor with split-screen works great and is more secure. Wi-Fi monitors are useful if you want to check the feed from your phone, but they introduce security risks and depend on your internet connection. If both babies are in the same room, a dedicated monitor is simpler.

What's the ideal camera angle for two cribs?

Mount each camera at a 45-degree angle above the crib, pointing down. This gives you a full view of the baby's face and body without obstruction. Avoid placing the camera directly above the crib—the angle is bad for seeing breathing movement.

How do I know if my router can handle two cameras?

Check your router's specs. You need at least 10 Mbps of upload bandwidth per camera. Most modern routers handle this fine, but if you have an older router (5+ years), test the connection before committing to a Wi-Fi monitor.

Bottom Line: What Should You Buy?

Buy the Infant Optics DXR-8 PRO with a second camera. It's the most reliable dual-camera setup I tested, with excellent night vision, seamless split-screen, and no Wi-Fi dependency. The $300 total cost is reasonable for the peace of mind it provides. If you're on a tight budget, the VTech VM901-2 is the best value at $190, but you'll lose the pan/tilt functionality. Skip the Nanit unless you specifically want breathing tracking—the subscription cost and Wi-Fi dependency aren't worth it for most parents. Two cameras are non-negotiable for twins.
