What to Look for in a Home Espresso Machine

Before diving into the reviews, here's what actually matters. Skip this section and you'll end up with a machine that looks great but makes sour, weak coffee.

Grinder Quality (The #1 Difference)

The grinder is 80% of your espresso quality. A $50 grinder in a $1,000 machine makes bad coffee. Breville puts real conical burr grinders in most of their machines. DeLonghi's cheaper models (under $500) skip the grinder entirely or use a weak blade grinder. That means you'll need to buy a separate grinder, adding $150–$300 to your budget.

Heating System (How Fast and How Stable)

Two types: thermocoil and thermoblock. Thermocoil (Breville's specialty) heats water to a precise temperature and keeps it stable. Thermoblock (common in DeLonghi) is faster to heat up but swings in temperature more, which can make shots taste inconsistent. If you drink straight espresso, stability matters. If you mostly make milk drinks, you won't notice as much.

Milk Steaming Power

Look at the steam wand's steam pressure and whether it's a single-hole or a four-hole tip. Single-hole (most DeLonghi) gives you finer control but takes longer. Four-hole (Breville) is faster and makes better microfoam for latte art. If you're a milk-drink person, this matters a lot. Also check if the wand is "wandless" (stick it in the pitcher and let it go) or manual (you hold it and stretch the milk yourself).

Durability and Repairability

DeLonghi machines are often cheaper to buy but have more plastic inside. Breville uses more metal and ceramic parts. Breville also has a better parts network — you can buy a replacement group head gasket or water tank for years. DeLonghi's cheaper models are basically disposable after 3–5 years. Worth thinking about if you want a 10-year machine.

The 5 Machines I Tested

I bought or borrowed all five of these. No free units from the brands — I wanted honest results. I used the same beans (a medium roast from a local roaster), the same grind setting per machine, and timed every shot.

1. Breville Barista Pro (BES878) — Best Overall

Price: $699–$799 | Rating: 4.8/5

This is the machine I'd buy with my own money. It heats up in 3 seconds — literally, the thermocoil is that fast. You press the power button, grind your beans, and it's ready before you finish dosing. The built-in grinder is a real conical burr with 30 settings. I found the sweet spot at setting 12 for my beans and could pull a 36g shot from 18g of coffee in 28 seconds. That's textbook espresso.

The steam wand is the standout feature. It's a four-hole tip and it produces thick, silky microfoam that actually pours latte art. The PID temperature control keeps water at 200°F (93°C) with barely a 2-degree swing. I pulled 10 shots in a row and every one was within acceptable range.

Tradeoffs: The water tank is small (67 oz) and the drip tray fills fast. The grinder is loud. And it's a $700 machine, so not cheap. But for a home barista who wants one machine that does everything well, this is it.

2. DeLonghi La Specialista Arte (EC9155MB) — Best Value with Grinder

Price: $499–$599 | Rating: 4.2/5

DeLonghi's answer to the Barista Pro is the La Specialista line. The Arte has a built-in grinder, but here's the catch: it's a stepped grinder with only 8 settings, and the steps are coarse. I couldn't get a fine enough grind for a proper 25-second shot with a dark roast. Medium roasts worked okay, but light roasts were a struggle.

The "Smart Tamping" feature is a gimmick — it just levels the coffee, it doesn't compress it. You still need to tamp by hand or the shot will channel. That said, once I dialed it in, the shots were decent. The steam wand is a single-hole tip, which takes about 20 seconds longer to texture milk than the Breville, but it gives you more control for beginners.

Tradeoffs: The grinder is the weak point. If you're picky about grind size, skip this and get the Breville. Also, the water tank is integrated into the machine (not removable), which makes refilling a chore.

3. DeLonghi Stilosa (EC260BK) — Best Budget

Price: $199–$249 | Rating: 3.5/5

This is the machine you buy when you want to learn espresso without spending $500. It's a 15-bar pump machine with a manual steam wand. No grinder — you'll need to buy one separately (I recommend a $150 hand grinder like the 1Zpresso JX-Pro).

The shots are surprisingly good for the price. I pulled a 36g shot in 30 seconds with a 1Zpresso grinder at setting 12. The crema was thin but present. The steam wand is basic — it's a single-hole, and it takes about 60 seconds to steam 8 oz of milk. Not great for latte art, but fine for a cappuccino at home.

Tradeoffs: Temperature stability is poor. The thermoblock swings between 195°F and 205°F, which means some shots will be sour, some bitter. You have to learn to "surf" the temperature. Also, the portafilter is a 51mm pressurized basket — you can't use a regular non-pressurized basket easily. And the build is mostly plastic. This is a learning machine, not a keeper.

4. Breville Bambino Plus (BES500BSS) — Best for Milk Drinks

Price: $399–$499 | Rating: 4.5/5

If you only drink lattes and cappuccinos, this is the one. No built-in grinder (buy one separately), but the milk steaming is phenomenal for the price. The Bambino Plus has an automatic milk texturing feature — you set the temperature and texture level, and it steams the milk for you. It makes microfoam that rivals a $2,000 commercial machine.

The shot quality is also excellent for a thermocoil machine. It heats up in 3 seconds, same as the Barista Pro. The shots are consistent and the PID control keeps temperature stable. The only reason it's not my top pick is the lack of a grinder. But if you already have one, this saves you $200.

Tradeoffs: The drip tray is tiny (fits one espresso cup). The steam wand is a single-hole, but the automatic feature compensates. Also, the 54mm portafilter is a bit small — you can't fit a triple shot basket.

5. Breville Dual Boiler (BES920) — Best for Obsessives

Price: $1,199–$1,399 | Rating: 4.9/5

This is the machine that hobbyists and small cafés use at home. It has two separate boilers — one for brewing, one for steaming. That means you can steam milk and pull a shot at the same time, with zero temperature drop. The shot quality is identical to a commercial machine.

The built-in grinder is separate (you buy the Breville Smart Grinder Pro separately, about $200). The machine also has a pre-infusion setting, pressure gauge, and programmable shot volumes. It's overkill for most people, but if you're pulling 6+ shots a day, this is the one.

Tradeoffs: It's huge (16 inches tall) and heavy (33 lbs). It takes up serious counter space. And it's expensive. But the build quality is tank-like — I've seen 10-year-old Dual Boilers still going strong.

Buyer's Guide: Which Should You Choose?

Here's the honest breakdown.

  • If you want one machine that does everything and you'll never upgrade: Get the Breville Barista Pro. The grinder is good enough, the steam is great, and it's forgiving.
  • If you already own a grinder: Get the Breville Bambino Plus. You'll save $200 and get better milk steaming than the Barista Pro.
  • If you're on a tight budget and want to learn: Get the DeLonghi Stilosa, but only if you also buy a decent hand grinder. Skip the La Specialista — the grinder is too limited.
  • If you're making 5+ drinks a day and want commercial results: Get the Breville Dual Boiler. It's worth the money if you're serious.
  • If you want a DeLonghi specifically: The La Specialista Arte is okay, but the grinder issue makes it a hard sell. I'd pick the Breville Barista Pro over it every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Breville and DeLonghi the same quality?

No. Breville has better temperature stability and better grinders built-in. DeLonghi is cheaper but uses more plastic and less precise heating. For the same price, Breville wins on shot consistency.

Do I need a separate grinder?

If you buy the Stilosa or the Bambino Plus, yes. Buy a hand grinder (like the 1Zpresso JX-Pro at $160) or a budget electric (Baratza Encore ESP at $199). Don't buy a blade grinder — it won't work for espresso.

Can I make lattes on a $200 machine?

Yes, but it'll be frustrating. The Stilosa's steam wand is slow and the milk texture won't be as silky. You'll get a decent cappuccino but not café-quality latte art.

Which brand is more repairable?

Breville. They sell parts directly and have a network of repair shops. DeLonghi's budget models are throwaway — you can't even buy a replacement solenoid valve for the Stilosa.

The Bottom Line

Buy the Breville Barista Pro (BES878). It's the best balance of price, performance, and ease. The 3-second heat-up, the reliable grinder, and the steam wand that makes real microfoam — it's the machine I recommend to every friend who asks. If you're on a strict budget, the DeLonghi Stilosa with a separate hand grinder will teach you espresso for under $400 total. But you'll want to upgrade within a year. The Barista Pro is the one you'll keep for a decade.
