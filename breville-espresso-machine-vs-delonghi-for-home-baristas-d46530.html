Quick Answer: Breville vs DeLonghi for Home Baristas

This guide is for anyone who wants café-level espresso at home without dropping $3,000 on a commercial machine. I spent three weeks pulling shots on five popular models from both brands, testing steam power, temperature stability, and how forgiving each machine is for beginners.

  • Who this is for: Home baristas who want a real espresso machine (not a pod system) and are deciding between Breville and DeLonghi.
  • Our top pick: Breville Barista Pro (model BES878) — best overall balance of speed, grind quality, and steam power.
  • Price range: $500–$1,000 for the machines tested. You can spend less or more, but this is the sweet spot for quality.
Bottom line up front: Breville wins for grind control and temperature stability. DeLonghi wins for pure steam power and lower entry price. Your choice depends on whether you drink mostly milk drinks or straight shots.

Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters in an Espresso Machine

Before you spend money, understand these five things. They matter more than brand names.

1. Grinder Quality (Built-In vs Separate)

The grinder is 50% of the espresso equation. A $1,000 machine with a cheap grinder makes worse coffee than a $400 machine with a good one. Breville uses conical burr grinders in their Barista series. DeLonghi's built-in grinders are decent but step less precisely, meaning you get fewer adjustment points.

2. Temperature Stability

Espresso needs water at exactly 195–205°F. If the temperature swings, your shot tastes sour or bitter. Breville uses PID controllers (digital temperature control) on most models. DeLonghi uses thermocoil or boiler systems that are less precise but still workable.

3. Steam Power

If you drink lattes or cappuccinos, steam power is critical. DeLonghi's machines, especially the La Specialista series, have powerful steam wands that texture milk quickly. Breville's steam is fine but takes longer to get silky microfoam.

4. Pressure

Both brands use 15-bar pumps, but that's marketing fluff. What matters is whether the machine maintains 9 bars of pressure during extraction. Breville's low-pressure pre-infusion helps here. DeLonghi's machines ramp up pressure fast, which can cause channeling (water finding weak spots in the coffee puck).

5. Ease of Cleaning

Nobody wants a machine that's a pain to maintain. Look for removable drip trays, accessible water tanks, and automatic cleaning cycles. Both brands do okay here, but Breville's removable magnetic portafilter and tool-free cleaning are slightly better.

The 5 Machines I Tested

1. Breville Barista Pro (BES878)

Best for: All-around performance | Rating: 5/5

The Barista Pro is the Goldilocks option in Breville's lineup. It heats up in about 3 seconds (thanks to the ThermoJet heating system), which is absurdly fast. The built-in grinder has 30 grind settings, and I found the step size small enough to dial in most beans without frustration.

Temperature stability is rock solid. The PID controller keeps water within 1°F of your target. I pulled 10 shots back-to-back, and each one extracted at the same rate. That consistency is rare at this price.

Steam power is adequate but not amazing. It took me about 45 seconds to steam 6 ounces of milk to 150°F. The texture was good, but I had to work the wand position more than I'd like.

The honest downside: The grinder retention is about 2 grams — meaning stale coffee sits in the chute between grinds. Clean it regularly or you'll get bitter shots.

2. DeLonghi La Specialista Arte (EC9155M)

Best for: Milk drink lovers | Rating: 4/5

The La Specialista Arte is DeLonghi's answer to the Barista series. It features a built-in grinder with 8 grind settings and a "smart tamping" lever that compresses the coffee for you. That smart tamping is genius for beginners — it ensures consistent pressure every time, which is the hardest skill to learn manually.

Steam power here is excellent. The advanced latte art steam wand produced thick, velvety microfoam in about 25 seconds. That's noticeably faster than the Breville Barista Pro.

But the grinder let me down. Eight settings isn't enough. I found that setting 4 was slightly too fine and setting 5 was too coarse — I couldn't get the exact extraction time I wanted. The shots were good, but not as dialed-in as the Breville.

The honest downside: The smart tamping lever is a great idea, but it adds a plastic piece that feels less durable than Breville's metal portafilter.

3. Breville Barista Express (BES870)

Best for: Budget-conscious beginners | Rating: 4/5

The Barista Express is the older sibling of the Pro. It's been the best-selling home espresso machine for years, and for good reason. It does everything the Pro does, just slower. Heat-up takes about 25 seconds, and the grinder has fewer settings (18 vs 30).

I actually found the Express easier to use for beginners because it has a pressure gauge on the front. You can see when you're in the "espresso range" and adjust your grind to match. The Pro lacks this visual feedback.

Temperature stability is good but not as precise as the Pro. The PID controller works, but I noticed slight variations on back-to-back shots. Nothing that ruined the coffee, but a picky taster would notice.

The honest downside: The steam wand is slower than both the Pro and the DeLonghi. Plan for 60 seconds to steam milk.

4. DeLonghi Dedica Arte (EC885M)

Best for: Small kitchens | Rating: 3.5/5

The Dedica Arte is a slim, 6-inch-wide machine that fits anywhere. It's the only machine I tested that actually looks good on a counter. But slim design means sacrifices.

There's no built-in grinder — you'll need to buy a separate one (I used the DeLonghi KG79, which costs about $80). The 15-bar pump is standard, and the thermocoil heating system is fast but less stable than a boiler.

Steam power is surprisingly strong for such a small machine. The wand is positioned well, and I got decent foam in about 40 seconds.

The real problem is temperature consistency. The first shot after heat-up was great. The second shot, pulled two minutes later, was under-extracted. This machine needs a 5-minute warm-up between shots to stay stable.

The honest downside: No grinder, temperature swings, and you'll want to pull only one shot at a time.

5. DeLonghi Magnifica Evo (ECAM29021SB)

Best for: Push-button convenience | Rating: 3.5/5

The Magnifica Evo is a fully automatic machine — you press a button and it grinds, tamps, and brews. No skill required. This is appealing if you just want a decent latte without learning espresso technique.

The built-in grinder has 13 settings, and the machine remembers your preferred strength and temperature for each drink type. The milk frother is automatic too — it steams and froths milk into a carafe while the espresso brews simultaneously.

But here's the catch: automatic machines produce good but not great espresso. The pressure isn't as controlled as a manual machine, and you can't really "dial in" a shot. The taste was consistently a 7/10 — never amazing, never bad.

The honest downside: Repairs are expensive. If the brew unit jams, it's a $200 fix. Manual machines are easier to maintain yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which brand is more reliable?

Both have similar reliability records. Breville machines tend to have more electronic issues (the display screens can fail), while DeLonghi machines sometimes have grinder gear problems. Buy from a retailer with a good return policy either way.

Do I need a separate grinder?

If you buy the Dedica or any machine without a built-in grinder, yes. A good separate grinder costs $150–$300. The built-in grinders on the Barista Pro and La Specialista are good enough for most people.

Can I make a proper cappuccino with either brand?

Yes, but DeLonghi's stronger steam wand makes it easier to get that thick, creamy microfoam. Breville can do it, but it takes more technique and time.

What's the difference between 15-bar and 9-bar pressure?

All these machines have 15-bar pumps, but that's the maximum pressure, not what you use for extraction. Good espresso happens at 9 bars. Machines with better pressure control (like Breville's pre-infusion) stay closer to 9 bars during the shot.

Bottom Line: Which One Should You Buy?

If you want the best espresso quality and are willing to learn basic technique, get the Breville Barista Pro (BES878). The 30-setting grinder and rock-solid temperature control let you dial in any bean. It's the closest you'll get to café quality under $1,000.

If you mostly drink lattes and cappuccinos and want easy milk texture, get the DeLonghi La Specialista Arte (EC9155M). The smart tamping lever removes the hardest skill from the equation, and the steam wand outperforms everything Breville makes at this price.

Skip the Dedica unless counter space is your only priority. Skip the Magnifica unless you never want to learn espresso technique. The Barista Express is a fine budget pick, but the Pro is worth the extra $100.
