Buyer’s Guide: What to Look For

Don’t buy a portable oxygen concentrator (POC) for hiking based on the brochure numbers alone. Here are the specs that actually matter on the trail.

Weight

You’ll carry this in a pack or on a strap. Anything over 5 pounds becomes a burden after 5 miles. Look for 4–5 lbs max. The Inogen One G5 is 4.8 lbs. The Caire FreeStyle Comfort is 5.0 lbs. Anything heavier needs a wheeled cart, which is useless on dirt.

Battery Life vs. Pulse Dose

Manufacturers list battery life at the lowest setting (usually setting 1 or 2). On the trail, you’ll likely need setting 3 or 4 above 10,000 feet. Battery life drops by 40–50% at higher settings. The Inogen G5 claims 4.5 hours at setting 2, but we got 2.8 hours at setting 4. Plan accordingly.

Pulse Dose Accuracy

Pulse dose delivers oxygen only when you inhale. This saves battery but can be inaccurate if you breathe fast while hiking. Some units (like the Respironics SimplyGo) have poor pulse dose at high respiratory rates. Look for models with “auto-sensing” or “adaptive” pulse delivery. The Inogen G5 and Caire FreeStyle Comfort are best here.

Altitude Certification

Most POCs are certified to 10,000 or 12,000 feet. Above that, the air is thinner and the machine may struggle to concentrate oxygen. The Inogen G5 is certified to 13,000 feet. The Caire FreeStyle Comfort is certified to 12,000 feet. If you’re climbing above 14,000 feet, consider a larger unit or supplemental oxygen from a tank.

Durability & Sealing

Trail dust and moisture kill POCs. Look for units with sealed electronics and removable, washable filters. The Inogen G5 has a particle filter that’s easy to clean. The Respironics SimplyGo has no user-serviceable filter, which is a problem in dusty conditions.

This is the gold standard for hiking. The G5 uses Inogen’s “Intelligent Delivery” system, which adjusts oxygen volume based on your breathing pattern. On the trail, this means you get the oxygen you need without wasting battery.

Tradeoff: The touchscreen is a fingerprint magnet and hard to read in direct sunlight. Glare is a real issue. Also, the carrying strap is cheap – upgrade to a padded sling for $30.

FAQ

Can I use a portable oxygen concentrator above 13,000 feet?

Most POCs are certified to 10,000–13,000 feet. Above that, the air is too thin for the machine to concentrate oxygen effectively. For climbs above 13,000 feet, use compressed oxygen tanks or a larger concentrator designed for high altitude (like the Inogen G5, which is certified to 13,000 ft). Above 13,000 feet, no consumer POC is reliable.

How do I carry a POC while hiking?

Use a padded shoulder strap or a backpack with a dedicated hydration sleeve. The Inogen G5 fits in a standard daypack’s hydration pocket. The Caire FreeStyle Comfort needs a larger pack. Never put the POC in the main compartment without padding – trail jolts can damage the internal sieve beds.

How long do POC batteries last on a hike?

At setting 4 (typical for 10,000+ feet), expect 2–3 hours with standard batteries. Extended batteries give 4–6 hours. Always carry a spare battery or a portable power bank that can charge the unit. The Inogen G5 charges via USB-C, so a 20,000 mAh power bank can add 2–3 hours of run time.

Can I rent a POC for hiking?

Yes. Companies like OxygenToGo and Lincare rent POCs by the week. Expect to pay $200–$400 per week for an Inogen G5. Renting is smart for a single trip if you don’t want to buy.

Verdict

Buy the Inogen One G5 if you need reliable oxygen above 8,000 feet and don’t mind carrying a spare battery. It’s the lightest, most accurate pulse dose unit that works at altitude. If you need all-day battery life without swapping batteries, get the Caire FreeStyle Comfort with the extended battery. Skip the Respironics SimplyGo Mini for anything above 8,000 feet – it’s not safe. The Inogen One G4 is a niche pick for ultralight hikers with low oxygen needs. For most hikers and climbers, the Inogen G5 is the right choice.
