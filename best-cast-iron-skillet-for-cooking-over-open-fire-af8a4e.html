Most skillets die over an open fire. Non-stick coatings peel. Thin stainless warps. Aluminum melts if you look at it wrong. Cast iron is the only real answer. But not all cast iron is the same. I've spent the last month cooking over wood fires, charcoal, and gas burners to find which pans hold up, which ones heat evenly, and which ones are a pain to handle when the handle gets hot enough to fry an egg.

Here’s what matters: weight, handle length, and pre-seasoning quality. A heavy pan holds heat better. A long handle keeps your knuckles from burning. Good factory seasoning saves you hours of work before your first trip. I tested five pans that cover the spectrum from cheap to premium. Here’s what I found.

What to Look For in a Campfire Skillet

Before you buy, understand these four things. They make or break a pan over fire.

Weight and Thickness

Thicker iron means more thermal mass. A thin pan gets hot spots and cools down the second you add food. Look for a pan that weighs at least 5 pounds for a 10-inch model, closer to 7-8 pounds for a 12-inch. The Lodge 12-inch weighs 7.8 pounds. That bulk is why it sears a steak perfectly and then keeps simmering beans without scorching.

Handle Design

Short stubby handles are a trap. They put your hand too close to the fire. You want a long handle, at least 7 inches, that lets you grip safely. Some pans come with a helper handle on the opposite side. Over a fire, you'll need that second hand—a 12-inch pan full of food is heavy and awkward to lift with one grip.

Seasoning Quality

Factory seasoning varies. Lodge uses vegetable oil, which works fine but needs a few cooks to build up. Higher-end brands like Field Company use multiple layers of grapeseed oil that create a slicker surface out of the box. For campfire use, you'll be re-seasoning the bottom anyway because direct flames burn off seasoning. Don't overpay for a fancy finish you'll degrade on the first fire.

Lid Compatibility

If you want to bake bread or cook stews, you need a lid. Some skillets come with domed lids that fit snugly. Others don't. Check the model number and buy a compatible lid at the same time. The Camp Chef 12-inch skillet has a matching lid that turns it into a mini Dutch oven. That's a big deal for campfire baking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a regular cast iron skillet over an open fire?

Yes. Any cast iron skillet can go over a fire. The enamel-coated ones cannot—the enamel will crack. Plain, bare cast iron is fine. The seasoning on the bottom will burn off, but that's normal. Just re-season the pan after your trip.

How do I keep food from sticking over a fire?

Heat the pan slowly. Don't put it directly over a roaring flame. Let the coals burn down to a consistent heat. Then add oil with a high smoke point—canola, grapeseed, or avocado oil. Let the oil shimmer before adding food. A well-seasoned pan helps too.

How do I clean a cast iron skillet after campfire cooking?

Scrape out food with a metal spatula while the pan is still warm. Rinse with hot water—no soap. If food is stuck, boil a little water in it to loosen the bits. Dry it completely over the fire or on a stove, then rub a thin layer of oil over the whole pan.

What's the best size for two people?

A 10-inch skillet is fine for two steaks or a small batch of hash. But a 12-inch gives you more flexibility. You can cook a whole meal in one pan—meat and veggies together. The extra weight is worth it.
