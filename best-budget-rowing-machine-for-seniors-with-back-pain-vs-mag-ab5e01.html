Why Magnetic Resistance Wins for Bad Backs

Rowing machines use four resistance types. Here's the short version for your spine:

  • Air/Piston: The pull gets harder the faster you go. It's jerky at the start of each stroke. Bad for a sensitive back.
  • Water: Smooth but expensive. Budget water rowers are cheaply made and slosh loudly.
  • Magnetic: Uses magnets to create resistance with zero friction. The pull is constant and silent. You control the intensity with a dial. This is the safe choice.

Magnetic rowers also let you change resistance mid-workout without stopping. If your back flares up at level 5, you click down to level 2 while still rowing. That's a game-changer.

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For

Before you buy any rower, check these five things. Missing one of these can mean a sore back after 10 minutes.

1. Seat Height and Cushioning

You should not have to lower yourself onto the seat. Look for a seat height of at least 18 inches from the floor. The SF-RW5515 sits at 19 inches. A thin, hard seat is a deal-breaker. You want at least 2 inches of foam. You can always add a gel seat pad for $15, but start with a decent one.

2. Footboard Angle

Your feet should rest flat with your knees bent at about 90 degrees. If the footboard is too high, you'll round your lower back to reach the handle. Look for an adjustable footboard with straps that hold your heel down. Heel lift = back strain.

3. Resistance Range

Seniors don't need 16 levels of crushing resistance. You need 8 smooth levels that you can adjust mid-stroke. The first 3 levels should feel like a gentle walk, not a sprint.

4. Rail Length

If you're under 5'10", a short rail (30 inches) is fine. Taller? You need a 36-inch rail. If your knees hit the handle at the catch position, you'll compensate with your back. That's how injuries happen.

5. Frame Stability

A wobbly frame makes you tense up. You want a steel frame that weighs at least 60 pounds. Lighter frames slide across the floor and force you to brace with your core—which is exactly what you don't want when your back hurts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is rowing actually safe for a herniated disc?

Yes, if you use proper form and low resistance. The key is to push with your legs first, then lean back slightly, then pull with your arms. Never round your back. Start with 5 minutes at level 1. If you feel sharp pain, stop and consult your doctor. Dull muscle soreness is fine; sharp pain is not.

How often should a senior row?

Start with 3 days a week, 10 minutes each. Build up to 20 minutes over 4 weeks. Your back needs time to strengthen the supporting muscles. Listen to your body—if you're sore the next day, wait an extra day.

Do I need a mat under the rower?

Yes. A $25 exercise mat protects your floor and stops the rower from sliding. The Sunny models have rubber feet, but they still shift on hardwood.

What's the difference between 8 resistance levels and 12?

Nothing important for seniors. The levels just indicate how many detents are on the magnetic dial. The actual resistance range is similar. More levels just mean finer adjustment. Don't pay extra for 12 levels—you won't use most of them.
