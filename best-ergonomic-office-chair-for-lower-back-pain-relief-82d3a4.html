Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters for Lower Back Pain

Don't get distracted by headrests or leather upholstery. Here's what to check before you buy.

Lumbar Support Adjustability

Your lower back has a natural inward curve. A chair needs to fill that space. The best chairs have adjustable lumbar support — either a pad that moves up and down or a firmness dial. Fixed lumbar bumps are a gamble. If the bump hits the wrong spot, it pushes your spine forward and makes pain worse. Look for chairs with at least 2 inches of vertical lumbar adjustment.

Seat Depth

When you sit back, you should have 2–3 fingers of space between the back of your knee and the front edge of the seat. Too deep and the seat edge presses on the back of your legs, cutting off circulation. Too shallow and your thighs don't get support. Chairs with a sliding seat pan (usually 2–3 inches of travel) are essential for taller or shorter users.

Recline Tension and Locking

Leaning back takes pressure off your spinal discs. But you need to control it. A tension knob lets you adjust how much force it takes to recline. A lock lets you fix the angle. Without these, you either sit bolt upright all day or constantly fight the chair's spring.

Seat Cushion Firmness

Memory foam sounds nice, but it's often too soft. It sinks under your sit bones and doesn't support your pelvis. Look for a high-density foam or a mesh seat. Mesh breathes better and distributes weight evenly. If you prefer foam, it should be firm enough that you feel supported, not swallowed.

Armrest Adjustability

Not directly about your back, but if your arms hang unsupported, your shoulders slouch forward. That pulls on your lower back. Get armrests that adjust in height and width, and ideally pivot. You want your elbows at a 90-degree angle, resting lightly.

The 5 Chairs We Tested

1. Herman Miller Aeron (Remastered)

Price: $1,295–$1,795 | Rating: 5/5

The Aeron is the chair that started the ergonomic revolution. The Remastered version (released a few years back) improved the lumbar support and made it adjustable. The mesh seat is firm but not hard, distributing your weight so there are no pressure points.

The PostureFit SL lumbar support is the key feature. Instead of a bump, it uses two pads that press against your pelvis and lower spine. It feels like someone is gently supporting you from behind. You can adjust the tension to push more or less.

Weaknesses? The armrests feel a bit cheap compared to the rest of the chair. And the design hasn't changed much since 1994 — it looks like a high-end office chair, not a futuristic throne. The mesh can also be cold against bare legs in winter.

Who should buy: Anyone who can afford it. It's the most tested and proven ergonomic chair in history.

2. Steelcase Gesture

Price: $1,100–$1,500 | Rating: 4.5/5

The Gesture was designed to support people who use technology — laptops, tablets, phones — not just sit at a desk. The armrests are the star. They move in every direction: up, down, in, out, and pivot. You can position them perfectly for any task.

Lumbar support is a firm pad that moves up and down. It's not as fancy as the Aeron's, but it's effective. The seat cushion is a high-density foam that's firm enough to support long sitting sessions.

Weaknesses? It's heavy — 57 pounds. Moving it is a chore. The recline is also a bit stiff at first, requiring a break-in period. And the price is steep.

Who should buy: People who use multiple devices and need armrests that can adapt.

3. Steelcase Series 1

Price: $520–$650 | Rating: 4/5

The Series 1 is Steelcase's budget option, but it doesn't feel cheap. It has the same adjustable lumbar support as its bigger siblings, just with fewer adjustments overall. The seat depth slides, the armrests adjust in height and width, and the recline has tension control.

For lower back pain, the lumbar support is the same design as the Gesture — a firm pad that moves up and down. It works well. The seat cushion is slightly softer than the Gesture's, which some might prefer.

Weaknesses? The armrests don't pivot, so you can't angle them. The build quality isn't as tank-like as the Gesture. And the recline lock only has four positions, not infinite adjustability.

Who should buy: Budget-conscious buyers who still want genuine lumbar support, not a gimmick.

4. Herman Miller Embody

Price: $1,600–$1,900 | Rating: 4/5

The Embody looks like a space-age spider web. It's designed to keep your spine in a neutral position while you move. The backrest uses a pixelated support system that flexes with your body, and the seat is a mesh-like material that doesn't put pressure on your thighs.

For back pain, the key feature is the Backfit adjustment. It tilts the whole backrest to match your spine's curve. You turn a knob and the backrest conforms to you.

Weaknesses? There's no lumbar support. Herman Miller claims the backrest itself is the support, but some users miss an explicit lumbar bump. The seat is also quite firm — it takes weeks to break in. And it's the most expensive chair here.

Who should buy: People who move around a lot while sitting and want a chair that flexes with them.

5. Haworth Zody

Price: $700–$900 | Rating: 4/5

The Zody is often overlooked, but it's one of the best values in ergonomic seating. It has a unique asymmetrical lumbar support — you can adjust the left and right sides independently. This is great for people with scoliosis or one-sided back pain.

It also has a pelvic support system that tilts the front of the seat slightly downward, which helps keep your pelvis in a neutral position. The seat depth adjusts, and the armrests move in and out.

Weaknesses? The lumbar support feels a bit flimsy compared to the Steelcase chairs. The recline tension knob is hard to reach. And the seat cushion is on the firm side.

Who should buy: People with one-sided back pain or scoliosis who need asymmetric support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my back hurt even with a good chair?

Because you're not moving. No chair can fix 8 hours of static sitting. Even the best chair only helps if you get up every 45–60 minutes. Set a timer and stand up, stretch, or walk for 2 minutes.

Is mesh or foam better for lower back pain?

Mesh is better for weight distribution and airflow. It doesn't sink like foam, so your spine stays in a better position. But foam can be more comfortable if you're very light or very heavy. Try both.

Do I need a headrest for back pain?

No. A headrest doesn't support your lower back. In fact, if you lean back into a headrest, you're more likely to slouch. Skip it unless you recline for long periods.

Will a chair fix my herniated disc?

No. A chair can prevent pain from getting worse, but it can't treat a medical condition. See a doctor or physical therapist for a herniated disc.

How long should an ergonomic chair last?

10–12 years for the high-end brands like Herman Miller and Steelcase. The cheaper chairs (under $300) usually last 2–3 years before the gas cylinder or foam gives out.

Bottom Line

Buy the Herman Miller Aeron (Remastered) if you can stomach the price. It's the most tested, most proven chair for lower back pain relief, and it'll last a decade or more. If you can't spend $1,300, get the Steelcase Series 1. It has the same lumbar support design as the $1,500 Gesture for half the price. Skip anything under $400 — it won't have the adjustability you need to fix your back. And remember: even the best chair can't replace standing up and moving around.
