Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters for Knee Pain

Before you drop two grand, here is what you need to know. Not all exercise bikes are created equal when your knees are the boss.

1. The Q-Factor (Crank Arm Width)

This is the distance between your pedals. Most bikes have a Q-factor of about 170mm. Peloton is 170mm. The S22i is slightly wider at 178mm.

Why does this matter? If your knees track outward when you pedal (common with knock-knees or hip issues), a wider stance feels better. If your knees track straight, the wider width can cause hip rocking that irritates your IT band. I have mild patellar tracking issues, and the wider stance on the S22i felt more natural. My friend, who has zero knee issues, hated the S22i's width and found it awkward. Test your own stance at a gym first.

2. Incline and Decline (The Game Changer)

The S22i tilts from -10% to +20%. The Peloton is flat, always flat.

Why this matters for knees: When you pedal on a flat road, you use the same muscle fibers in a repetitive motion. That repetition can flare up patellar tendinitis. When you add incline, you shift the load to your glutes and hamstrings. When you hit decline (downhill), you engage your quads differently and give your patellar tendon a break from the grind. Variety in joint angle reduces strain. The S22i gives you that variety automatically during their scenic rides. The Peloton cannot do this, period.

3. Resistance Type: Magnetic vs. Electromagnetic

Both use magnetic resistance, which is smooth and quiet. Peloton uses a manual magnetic brake. The S22i uses an electromagnetic brake that auto-adjusts.

For knee pain, the Peloton's manual knob is actually better for one reason: you can micro-adjust the resistance. If you feel a twinge, you can dial it back in half a turn. The S22i's auto-resistance changes in set increments, which can sometimes be too much at once. However, the S22i has a manual mode too, so you can override it.

4. Saddle and Geometry

Both bikes come with saddles that are bricks. You will need a gel cover or a new saddle on either. The geometry is similar—both are road-bike style, which puts you in a forward-leaning position. If you have lower back pain along with knee pain, the more upright S22i posture is easier on your spine.

5. The Subscription Trap

Peloton requires a $44/month membership to unlock the bike's features. Without it, you get a "free ride" mode with no metrics. The S22i requires a $39/month iFit membership for auto-incline, scenic rides, and structured workouts. Without iFit, you get a manual bike with a basic screen. Both are useless without the subscription, so factor that into the cost. $500/year on top of the bike.

Individual Product Reviews

NordicTrack Commercial S22i Studio Cycle

Price: $1,999 (often on sale for $1,499–$1,799) | Rating: 4.5/5

The S22i is the clear winner for knee pain, and here is why: the -10% to +20% incline capability is not a gimmick. When I rode the "Alps" scenic route, the bike auto-tilted to match the terrain. Going downhill at -5% took immediate pressure off my patellar tendon. Going uphill at 12% forced me to sit back and use my glutes, which stabilized my knee joint.

The 22-inch rotating touchscreen is massive and crisp. The iFit classes are filmed in real locations (Costa Rica, Swiss Alps, Iceland) and the bike adjusts resistance and incline to match the video. It feels like you are there.

The downsides: The bike is heavy (205 lbs) and hard to move. The iFit software can be laggy on startup. The auto-resistance changes are sometimes jarring—it jumped 12 points once and I felt a pop in my knee. You have to watch the "Effort Score" and manually override if it turns up too fast. Also, the seat post creaks after 3 months. Annoying but not disabling.

Knee verdict: The incline variety is the single best feature for knee pain. I recommend this for anyone with patellar issues, runner's knee, or arthritis.

Peloton Bike (Original)

Price: $1,445 | Rating: 4/5

The Peloton Bike is the cultural icon. The classes are the best in the industry—period. The instructors (Sam Yo, Hannah Frankson) are engaging, and the leaderboard adds a competitive element that makes you push harder. But for knee pain, it has a fatal flaw: no incline.

You only have resistance. That means your knee joint angle never changes. Every pedal stroke is identical for 30 minutes. For me, by minute 20, my right knee started aching from the repetitive angle. I had to constantly stand up out of the saddle (which is hard on knees) just to change the load.

However, the manual resistance knob is precise. You can dial in the exact resistance you need to avoid pain. The bike is also rock solid—no wobble, no creaks. The 21.5-inch screen is non-rotating (but the new ones have a swivel mount).

The downsides: The stock saddle is a medieval torture device. You will spend $100+ on a new one. The subscription is $44/month and the classes are indoor-studio only—no scenic rides. The Q-factor is 170mm, which is fine for most but not for wide-stance riders.

Knee verdict: If you are disciplined about staying in the saddle and using low resistance with high cadence (90 RPM+), the Peloton can work. But it is not ideal.

Peloton Bike+

Price: $2,895 | Rating: 4.5/5

The Bike+ adds four things over the original: a rotating 23.8-inch screen, auto-resistance (based on the instructor's callouts), a better sound system, and Apple GymKit integration. The auto-resistance is a double-edged sword for knee pain.

When a class calls for "resistance 40–50," the bike adjusts itself. If the instructor says "add 10 resistance" and you are already at 35, the bike jumps to 45. That sudden 10-point jump can be a knee killer. You can turn off auto-resistance and use the manual knob (which is still there), but then you paid $1,000 extra for nothing.

The rotating screen is a big deal. You can do off-bike strength classes, which is actually better for knee pain than cycling. Strengthening your glutes and hips takes load off the knee joint. The Bike+ is the only one on this list that makes off-bike workouts seamless.

The downsides: Price. And the same flat-road limitation as the original. No incline, no decline, no terrain variety.

Knee verdict: Only buy this if you plan to use the strength and bootcamp classes. If you are purely cycling, the original is better value.

NordicTrack S15i Studio Cycle

Price: $1,599 | Rating: 3.5/5

The S15i is the S22i's little brother. Same incline/decline (-10% to +20%), same iFit integration, but a smaller 15-inch screen and a slightly lighter flywheel (32 lbs vs. 32 lbs—actually the same). The main difference is the screen size and the price.

For knee pain, it offers the same benefits as the S22i: terrain variety, auto-incline, and the ability to shift load between muscle groups. The smaller screen is actually fine for cycling—you are looking at the road, not the screen.

The downsides: The screen is non-rotating, so off-bike workouts are awkward. The iFit software is the same laggy experience. And at $1,599, you are only $400 away from the S22i, which has a much better screen. The value proposition is weak unless you catch a massive sale.

Knee verdict: Buy this only if you find it for under $1,200. Otherwise, get the S22i.

FAQ: Knee Pain and Exercise Bikes

Is cycling good for knee arthritis?

Yes. Cycling is a low-impact, non-weight-bearing exercise that lubricates the knee joint and strengthens the quadriceps, which support the knee. The key is to avoid high resistance and keep your cadence above 80 RPM. The S22i's decline feature is especially helpful for arthritis because it reduces the angle of knee flexion.

Should I get a recumbent bike instead?

If you have severe knee pain (e.g., post-surgery or advanced osteoarthritis), a recumbent bike (like the Schwinn 270) is better because it puts your legs out in front, reducing compression on the joint. The Peloton and S22i are upright bikes, which require more knee flexion. If you can't bend your knee past 90 degrees without pain, get a recumbent. If you can, the S22i is fine.

How do I set up the bike to avoid knee pain?

Three rules: (1) Seat height should allow a slight bend (about 25 degrees) at the knee at the bottom of the pedal stroke—your leg should never fully straighten. (2) Seat fore-aft position: when the pedal is at 3 o'clock, the front of your knee should be directly over the pedal axle. (3) Cleat position: the ball of your foot should be over the pedal spindle. Get this wrong on any bike and you will have pain.

Can I use the Peloton without a subscription for knee rehab?

Yes, but it is barebones. You get a "Just Ride" mode with no metrics on the screen, no resistance guidance, and no classes. You can still manually adjust resistance. But honestly, if you are doing rehab, you need structured guidance. The iFit subscription on the S22i includes "Rehab" and "Low Impact" ride collections that are specifically designed for joint issues. Peloton's "Low Impact" classes exist but are not as structured for injury recovery.

Which has better customer service for warranty claims?

Both are mediocre. Peloton has a better app and community, but their hardware support is slow (2–3 week wait times for parts). NordicTrack is known for long hold times on the phone, but they send parts faster. For knee pain, the frame warranty matters: Peloton offers 5 years on the frame, 1 year on parts. NordicTrack offers 10 years on the frame, 2 years on parts. NordicTrack wins.

Bottom Line Verdict

Buy the NordicTrack S22i if you have knee pain. It is not the best bike overall—the Peloton's classes and community are superior—but the -10% to +20% incline and decline is a genuine, medically-sensible feature for joint relief. It lets you change the angle of your knee joint throughout a ride, which prevents the repetitive stress that causes patellar pain. The wider Q-factor also helps if you have hip or tracking issues. The Peloton Bike+ is the better investment if you plan to do strength training to fix the root cause of your knee pain (weak glutes), but for purely cycling with knee issues, the S22i wins by a mile. Just budget for the $39/month iFit subscription and a new saddle.
