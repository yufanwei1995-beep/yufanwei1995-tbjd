If your kitchen is short on counter space, the last thing you need is another bulky appliance hogging the real estate. But you also want nugget ice for your iced coffee, and you don't want to wrestle with trays in the freezer. A countertop ice maker solves that, but they aren't all created equal. Some are slow, some are loud, and some just don't fit where you need them.

This guide is for anyone with a small kitchen who wants a dedicated ice maker without sacrificing their entire countertop. I’ve tested dozens of these units over the years, and I’ve narrowed it down to the ones that actually earn their spot. I’m going to tell you what specs matter, which models are worth your money, and which ones you should skip.

Quick Answer: The Best Countertop Ice Makers for Small Kitchens

Who this is for: People with limited counter space who want a steady supply of chewable ice without a plumbed-in machine.

Our top pick: GE Profile Opal 1.0 (Gen 2) — it's the gold standard for nugget ice, and the newer model is quieter and smaller than the original.

Price range: You'll spend between $100 for a basic cube maker and $600 for a premium nugget unit. The sweet spot for quality and size is $200–$350.

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For

Before you hit "add to cart," you need to understand that "ice maker" is a broad category. Here are the four things that matter most when you're working with a small countertop.

1. Physical Footprint

Measure your space first. A standard unit is about 12 inches wide, 16 inches deep, and 15 inches tall. That's roughly the size of a microwave. The GE Profile Opal 1.0 is 11.5"W x 16.5"D x 15.5"H, which fits under most upper cabinets. If you have less than 12 inches of width, you're looking at a portable cooler-style unit, which is smaller but makes less ice.

2. Ice Type: Nugget vs. Bullet

This is the biggest decision. Nugget ice (Sonic-style) is soft, chewable, and absorbs flavor. It's what most people want, but it requires a more complex machine. Bullet ice is hard, clear, and slower to melt in drinks. Bullet makers are cheaper and smaller, but if you buy one expecting nugget ice, you'll be disappointed.

3. Production Speed and Capacity

Look at the 24-hour output, not the "first batch" time. Most units make 26–40 lbs per day. That sounds like a lot, but the internal storage bin only holds about 1.5–2 lbs. That means the machine cycles: makes ice, melts it to keep the bin from overflowing, then makes more. If you're hosting a party, you'll need to empty the bin into a freezer bag to keep up with demand.

4. Noise Level

These are not quiet appliances. The compressor in a nugget maker is loud. The GE Profile Opal is noticeably quieter than the older model, but it still hums constantly. If you have an open-concept kitchen and a TV in the same room, you'll hear it. If that's a dealbreaker, look for a unit with a "quiet mode" or a lower decibel rating.

5. Water Source

You have two options: pour water into a reservoir, or plumb it into your line. For a small kitchen, a reservoir is the way to go. The Opal has a 72-oz reservoir that takes 4–5 refills a day if you're making a lot of ice. Some cheaper units have smaller reservoirs, which means more trips to the sink.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a countertop ice maker take to make ice?

Most units make the first batch in 6–15 minutes. The GE Profile Opal takes about 15 minutes because it needs to cool the compressor down first. Budget bullet makers are faster because they don't have to pre-cool as much.

Do countertop ice makers need a water line?

No. They all use a pour-in reservoir. You fill the tank, the machine pulls water from it, and you refill as needed. The Opal has a side tank that's easy to access, while the Euhomy's is on the top, which is slightly more awkward.

Is nugget ice worth the extra cost?

It depends on how much you like chewing ice. Nugget ice is softer, absorbs the flavor of your drink, and doesn't stick together in clumps. If you mainly use ice for iced coffee or cocktails, the extra cost is worth it. If you just want to cool water on a hot day, bullet ice is perfectly fine.

Why does my ice maker melt the ice?

It's normal. The machines can't store much ice, so they melt it periodically to prevent the bin from overflowing. This is called a "harvest cycle." If your ice is melting too fast, check the ambient temperature — these units don't work well in hot, humid kitchens.

If you want the best countertop ice maker for a small kitchen and you're willing to spend the money, get the GE Profile Opal 1.0 (Gen 2). It makes the best ice, it's quieter than the old model, and it's compact enough for most counters.
