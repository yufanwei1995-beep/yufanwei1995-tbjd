What to Look For in a Digestive Enzyme (Buyer's Guide)

Not all enzyme pills are created equal. Most cheap ones just contain a basic amylase and protease blend — that's like bringing a butter knife to a steak dinner. Here’s the breakdown of what actually matters.

1. Enzyme Variety (The Big Three Plus)

You need a formula that covers all three macronutrients. Protease breaks down protein, amylase handles carbs, and lipase breaks down fats. If you bloat after any meal, you need all three. But you also want the specialty guys: Alpha-galactosidase (breaks down the sugars in beans and broccoli) and Lactase (breaks down milk sugar). If you're sensitive to gluten, look for DPP-IV, which helps break down gluten peptides.

2. Potency (Look at the Units, Not the Milligrams)

Enzymes are measured in "activity units" (like FCC, HUT, DU, or ALU), not milligrams. A 500mg capsule of a weak enzyme is worthless. You want a label that lists specific activity levels. For example, a good lipase activity is over 3,000 FCC units. If the label just says "Proprietary Blend 250mg" without listing the enzymes, put it back on the shelf.

3. Delivery Mechanism (Capsule vs. Tablet)

You want a capsule that dissolves in your stomach, not an enteric-coated tablet. Enteric coating is designed to bypass the stomach and dissolve in the intestines — that's great for some meds, but bad for enzymes. You need the enzymes to mix with food in the stomach's acidic environment to get the job done. Capsules are the standard for this reason.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I take digestive enzymes?

Take them right before you start eating, or with the first bite. The enzymes need to mix with the food in your stomach. Taking them after a meal is like calling a fire truck after the house has burned down.

Can I take them every day?

Yes. Most experts agree it's safe for long-term use. However, think of enzymes as a crutch, not a cure. If you rely on them every single day, your body might start producing fewer of its own enzymes. Use them strategically for trigger meals, but also work on gut health with probiotics and a varied diet.

Will they help with IBS (Irritable Bowel Syndrome)?

They can help manage the symptoms. A lot of IBS bloating is caused by FODMAPs — fermentable carbs. Enzymes containing Alpha-galactosidase and Lactase break down those specific sugars, reducing the fermentation that causes gas. They won't cure IBS, but they can make eating much more comfortable.

Why do I feel worse after taking them?

You might be taking too high a dose. High-potency protease (the protein enzymes) can sometimes cause a burning sensation in the stomach or esophagus if you don't have enough food to digest. Start with half a capsule and work your way up. Also, if you're not drinking enough water, enzymes can't work properly — they need water to activate.
