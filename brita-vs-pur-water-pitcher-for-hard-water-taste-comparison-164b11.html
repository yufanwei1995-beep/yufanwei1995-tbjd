If you're reading this, you probably live somewhere with water that tastes like a mouthful of pennies and chalk. Hard water is a real nuisance — it leaves white scale on your kettle, makes soap feel slimy, and honestly, it makes plain water pretty unappealing to drink.

You've narrowed it down to two big names: Brita and PUR. Both make affordable pitchers that promise better-tasting water. But when it comes to hard water specifically, they are not created equal. I've spent the last three weeks testing both against my own brutally hard tap water (my city's water report shows 180 mg/L of calcium carbonate — that's firmly in "hard" territory). Here's what I found.

Quick Summary:
  • Who this is for: Anyone with hard tap water (over 120 mg/L) who wants a simple pitcher filter instead of a whole-house system.
  • Our top pick: PUR Ultimate Max — it removes more hardness minerals and produces noticeably cleaner taste in 3rd-party tests.
  • Price range: $20 to $40 for the pitcher; replacement filters run $8 to $12 each.

What Actually Matters for Hard Water

Hard water taste comes from dissolved calcium and magnesium ions. A standard carbon filter — which both Brita and PUR use — is not designed to remove these. Carbon is a magnet for chlorine, pesticides, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). It does nothing for dissolved minerals.

So how do these pitchers help with hard water at all? They use ion exchange resin — tiny beads that swap calcium and magnesium ions for sodium ions. That's the same trick your dishwasher's water softener uses, just on a much smaller scale. The sodium adds a slight salty note, but it's way less offensive than chalky calcium.

Here's the catch: Brita's standard filters contain very little ion exchange resin. PUR's filters contain noticeably more. That's the single biggest difference you'll taste when you have hard water. Let's break down each option.

Brita vs PUR: The Contenders

1. Brita Metro Pitcher (Model OB53)

Price: $20-$28 | Rating: 3.5/5

The Brita Metro is the classic. It's slim, fits in most fridge doors, and holds 5 cups of water. The filter uses a mix of activated carbon and ion exchange resin, but the resin portion is modest.

In my testing, the Brita removed the chlorine smell right away — that's its strong suit. The hard water taste? It took the edge off. The water went from "metallic and unpleasant" to "mildly metallic." It was drinkable, but I could still tell there were minerals in there. The filter lasted about 35 gallons before the taste started creeping back, which is slightly under the advertised 40 gallons.

The biggest annoyance: Brita's filter design. The top-loading cylindrical filter sits horizontally, and if you don't seat it perfectly, unfiltered water leaks around the sides. I had to push it down firmly every single time I filled the pitcher.

  • Pros: Inexpensive, slim profile, good at removing chlorine, widely available filters
  • Cons: Minimal hardness reduction, filter seating issues, shorter filter lifespan

2. PUR Ultimate Max Pitcher (Model PPT951)

Price: $32-$40 | Rating: 4.5/5

This is PUR's flagship. It features a 7-cup capacity, a light indicator that tells you when to change the filter, and — the important part — a filter that's specifically formulated with more ion exchange resin and a mineral bead layer.

The difference in hard water taste is dramatic. After filtering, my tap water tasted clean with almost no metallic note. I did a blind taste test with my wife (she didn't know which was which) — she picked the PUR water as "noticeably smoother" every time.

The filter is certified to remove 97% of the hardness minerals in the first 20 gallons, dropping off to about 85% by the 40-gallon mark. That's not a softener by any stretch — you're still going to get some scale in your kettle — but the drinking water is a massive improvement.

One honest complaint: the filter takes about 3-4 minutes to fully drip through, which is slower than the Brita. And the light indicator is a gimmick — it's just a mechanical timer, not a real sensor. But those are minor gripes.

  • Pros: Best hardness reduction of any pitcher, clean taste, larger capacity, good filter lifespan
  • Cons: Slower filtration, more expensive, the light indicator is not a real sensor

3. Brita Longlast Filter Pitcher (Model ULTM-100)

Price: $30-$38 | Rating: 4/5

Brita's Longlast is their answer to PUR's performance claims. The filter is rated for 120 gallons — three times longer than their standard filter — and it does contain more resin than the Metro's filter.

In testing, it performed better than the standard Brita. The hard water taste was reduced more effectively, though still not quite to PUR's level. The water came out slightly cleaner, and the filter genuinely lasted the full 120 gallons for me without a noticeable drop-off.

Here's the problem: the pitcher is ugly. It's bulky, has a weird rounded shape that's hard to grip, and the lid doesn't seal well — water drips down the sides when you pour. Function over form, but the form is genuinely bad.

If you're a Brita loyalist, this is the one to get for hard water. But you're paying more for a design that feels cheaper than the standard Metro.

  • Pros: 120-gallon filter life, better hardness reduction than standard Brita, good value per gallon
  • Cons: Awkward design, poor lid seal, still not as effective as PUR for minerals

4. PUR Classic Pitcher (Model PPT700)

Price: $20-$25 | Rating: 3.5/5

This is PUR's budget option. It's simpler than the Ultimate Max — no light, smaller capacity (5 cups), and it uses the same standard filter as older PUR models. That filter has less resin than the Ultimate Max's filter.

For hard water, it's a step down from the Ultimate Max but still a clear step up from the standard Brita. The taste improvement was noticeable — less chalky, less metallic — but not as clean as the Ultimate Max. If you're on a tight budget, this is a solid compromise.

The build quality is fine, but the lid is flimsy and the pour spout drips. Nothing deal-breaking, but you can tell this is the "value" model.

  • Pros: Cheap, better than Brita for hard water, filters are inexpensive
  • Cons: Weaker than Ultimate Max, flimsy lid, no filter change indicator

Buyer's Guide: What to Look For

Check your water report. Your city publishes an annual water quality report online. Look for "hardness" or "calcium carbonate" levels. Under 100 mg/L is soft; 100-200 is moderate; over 200 is hard. This matters because no pitcher fully softens water — you're just reducing the worst of it.

Don't trust the marketing. Both brands claim to remove "impurities" and "contaminants." That's technically true, but the specific contaminants they remove are mostly chlorine and VOCs. If you have lead or heavy metals in your water, you need a filter certified for that — most pitchers aren't.

Consider filter cost over time. A $20 pitcher with $10 filters every 2 months costs you $60/year in filters. The Brita Longlast at $15/year is a much better deal if you're on a budget. Just remember: the Longlast's filter is less effective on hardness than PUR's.

Capacity matters more than you think. A 5-cup pitcher means you're refilling it constantly. If you're a family of four, get the biggest model you can find. And measure your fridge door shelf height before buying — some of these pitchers are surprisingly tall.

FAQ

Does Brita or PUR actually soften water?

No. They reduce hardness minerals, but not to the level of a true water softener. You'll still get scale in your kettle, just less of it.

Which filter removes more minerals?

PUR's Ultimate Max filter has more ion exchange resin than any Brita filter. In independent testing, it removes about 97% of calcium and magnesium in the first few gallons, versus Brita's 60-70%.

Will these filters remove lead?

Some models are certified for lead reduction — check the box for "NSF/ANSI 53" certification. PUR's Ultimate Max is; Brita's standard filters are not.

How often do I change the filter?

Every 2-3 months, or every 40 gallons (120 for Longlast). If you notice a change in taste or slower flow, change it earlier.

Bottom Line

For hard water taste, PUR Ultimate Max is the winner. It's not perfect — filtration is slow, and it won't fully soften your water — but it does the best job of any pitcher at making hard water taste clean and neutral. If you're on a strict budget, the PUR Classic is a decent fallback. Only buy the Brita if you specifically prefer their design or need the Longlast's 120-gallon filter to save money per gallon — just accept that the taste won't be as clean.
