What to Look For in a Hiking Boot for Plantar Fasciitis and Wide Feet

You cannot just buy any "wide" boot. Here is what matters most if you have plantar fasciitis.

Arch Support

Plantar fasciitis is an inflammation of the ligament running along the bottom of your foot. Firm arch support reduces strain on that ligament. Look for boots with a built-in medial post or a shank that provides rigidity. If the boot feels floppy when you bend it, skip it.

Heel Drop and Cushioning

A heel-to-toe drop of 8-12mm is ideal for plantar fasciitis. It shifts pressure away from the heel and Achilles tendon. Too much cushioning can be bad. Soft foam feels nice in the store but collapses after 50 miles, leaving you with zero support. Look for mid-density foam like EVA or polyurethane.

True Wide Fit

Many brands claim "wide" but add only 2mm to the forefoot. Look for boots with a wide toe box (like Keen or Altra) or ones that offer actual EE widths (like Merrell or Hoka). Your toes should be able to spread out naturally. If your pinky toe touches the side of the boot, it is too narrow.

Removable Insole

This is critical. You will likely want to swap the stock insole for a custom orthotic or a high-quality insole like Superfeet Green or Powerstep. If the insole is glued in, you cannot do that. Always check that the insole comes out easily.

Weight

Heavy boots make you work harder and alter your gait, which can aggravate plantar fasciitis. Look for boots under 2 pounds (32 ounces) per boot. Anything heavier will tire your feet faster.

1. Keen Targhee III Waterproof Wide

The Keen Targhee III is the gold standard for wide feet. The toe box is famously roomy, shaped like a foot rather than a pointed triangle. This gives your toes room to spread, which reduces pressure on the plantar fascia.

The arch support is moderate but firm. It is not as aggressive as a custom orthotic, but it holds your foot in a neutral position. The insole is removable, so you can drop in a Superfeet Green insole for extra support.

2. Hoka Anacapa 2 Mid GTX Wide

Price: $195-$220 | Rating: 4.5/5

Hoka is known for max cushioning, but the Anacapa 2 has a surprisingly firm midsole. The EVA foam is denser than what you find in running shoes, which means it does not bottom out on rocky trails. This is good news for plantar fasciitis sufferers because soft foam actually increases strain on the plantar fascia.

The wide version offers a true EE width. The toe box is wide but not as spacious as Keen. The arch support is excellent — Hoka uses a J-Frame construction that cradles the arch and prevents overpronation.

The rocker sole is the star here. It propels you forward with each step, reducing the load on your heel. This made a huge difference for my heel pain on descents.

The downside: The ankle collar is stiff and takes about 10 miles to break in. Also, the lacing system is finicky — the laces are thin and can dig into your instep if you have high arches.

3. Merrell Moab 3 Mid Waterproof Wide

Price: $130-$150 | Rating: 4.2/5

The Moab is the best-selling hiking boot for a reason. It is affordable, comfortable, and available in a true wide width. The wide version measures 4E, which is genuinely wide. My wide-footed testers had zero complaints about toe pinching.

The arch support is medium. It is not as firm as the Hoka or Keen, but it is adequate for mild plantar fasciitis. The insole is removable, and the boot has a nylon arch shank that provides torsional rigidity.

The cushioning is plush but not mushy. The Merrell Air Cushion in the heel absorbs shock well, which helps with heel strike pain.

The downside: The leather upper is stiffer than the Keen and requires a longer break-in period. Also, the waterproofing (M Select DRY) is not as reliable as Gore-Tex. After heavy rain, my socks got damp.

4. Salomon X Ultra 4 Wide Mid GTX

Price: $160-$180 | Rating: 4.0/5

The Salomon X Ultra 4 is a lightweight option for hikers who want agility. At 1 pound 5 ounces per boot, it is the lightest on this list. The wide version offers a genuine wide fit, though the toe box is slightly tapered compared to Keen.

The arch support is firm and aggressive. This is great for plantar fasciitis but can be uncomfortable if you have flat feet or low arches. The insole is thin and not great quality — plan on replacing it.

The Active Chassis in the midsole provides stability without adding weight. The grip is exceptional, with deep lugs that bite into loose dirt and mud.

The downside: The Quicklace system is polarizing. It is convenient but if it breaks, you cannot replace it easily. Also, the toe box is not as tall as the Keen, so if you have very high-volume feet, this might not work.

Price: $185-$200 | Rating: 4.3/5

Oboz is a smaller brand, but they make excellent boots for foot issues. The Sawtooth X comes with the Oboz B-DRY waterproofing and a built-in insole called the O Fit Insole. This insole has a firm arch cookie that actually supports the medial arch.

The wide width is generous and the toe box allows natural toe splay. The midsole is a polyurethane blend that is firm and durable. These boots will last 500+ miles before the foam breaks down.

The heel cup is deep and locks your foot in place. This prevents heel slippage, which is crucial for plantar fasciitis because any friction on the heel aggravates the condition.

The downside: They are heavy at 1 pound 14 ounces. The break-in period is long — expect 20-30 miles before they feel comfortable.

Buyer's Guide: How to Choose the Right Boot for Your Feet

Do You Need an Orthotic?

If your plantar fasciitis is severe, you likely need a custom orthotic. In that case, the boot's insole matters less. You just need a boot with a removable insole and a deep enough footbed to accommodate the orthotic. The Keen and Oboz have the deepest footbeds.

Consider the Terrain

For rocky, uneven trails, get the Hoka Anacapa 2. The rocker sole and firm midsole will protect your feet. For smooth, well-maintained trails, the Merrell Moab 3 is plenty.

Don't Skimp on Socks

Your socks matter as much as your boots. Wear a wool blend sock with cushioning, like Darn Tough or Smartwool. Avoid cotton, which causes friction and blisters.

Break Them In Slowly

Even the best boot will hurt if you wear it on a 10-mile hike immediately. Wear them around the house for a week. Then do short 2-mile walks. Gradually increase the distance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I replace the insole in these boots?

Yes, all five boots have removable insoles. This is essential for plantar fasciitis because you will want to swap in a firmer insole like Superfeet Green or Powerstep Pinnacle.

Are these boots good for flat feet?

The Hoka and Oboz have the most aggressive arch support, which can be painful for flat feet. The Keen and Merrell are more neutral and better for flat feet.

How do I know if I need a wide width?

If your toes feel cramped in standard width boots, or if you get blisters on your pinky toe, you need a wide. You can also trace your foot on paper and measure the widest part. If it is over 4 inches, you likely need a wide.

What is the difference between men's and women's wide widths?

Women's wide is typically a D width, while men's wide is an E or EE. If you have very wide feet, consider buying men's sizes even if you are a woman, for the extra space.

Bottom Line Verdict

If you want the best all-around boot for wide feet and plantar fasciitis, buy the Keen Targhee III Waterproof Wide. It has the roomiest toe box, solid arch support, and requires almost no break-in. If your heel pain is severe, spend the extra money on the Hoka Anacapa 2 — the rocker sole genuinely reduces heel strike impact. Budget-conscious hikers can get the Merrell Moab 3 and add a firm aftermarket insole. Whatever you choose, replace the stock insole with a firm orthotic and break the boots in slowly. Your feet will thank you on the trail.
