Buyer’s Guide: What Matters for Hot Yoga Mats

Before we get into the showdown, you need to know what separates a good hot yoga mat from a bad one. Here’s what I look for:

Grip When Wet

This is everything. A mat that feels sticky when dry but turns slippery when you sweat is useless for hot yoga. The best mats use either polyurethane (PU) tops or textured rubber. These materials get more grippy when wet, not less.

Thickness and Cushioning

Thicker mats (5mm+) feel better on your knees and elbows. But they’re heavier and harder to balance on. Thinner mats (3mm) are lighter and give you better ground feel, but your joints will complain. Find the sweet spot for your body.

Durability

Hot yoga destroys mats. Sweat, heat, friction — it all breaks down cheap materials fast. Look for closed-cell surfaces that resist absorbing sweat and bacteria. Open-cell mats absorb everything and will smell like a gym sock in a month.

Weight and Portability

If you walk or bike to class, weight matters. A 4-pound mat feels great on your shoulder. An 8-pound mat feels like you’re carrying a body bag. Also check if it rolls up tightly or bulges.

Price vs. Value

You can get a decent mat for $30. You can get a great mat for $100+. The question is whether the extra cost gets you real benefits — better grip, longer life, better materials. Sometimes it does. Sometimes you’re paying for a logo.

Gaiam Premium Print Mat Review

Price: $22-$35 | Rating: 3.5/5 | Best for: Beginners and budget-focused yogis

The Gaiam Premium Print Mat is the mat you see everywhere. It’s cheap, colorful, and available at Target. But is it good for hot yoga? Let’s break it down.

Grip Performance

This mat has a PVC surface with a subtle texture. Dry, it’s fine — decent grip for basic poses. Add sweat, and things get dicey. In my first hot class, I noticed slipping in downward dog within 10 minutes. The mat doesn’t absorb moisture; it lets it pool on the surface. That’s dangerous for balance poses like Warrior III or Half Moon.

I’ll be blunt: if you sweat heavily, this mat will fail you. It’s not designed for wet conditions. Gaiam markets it as a general-purpose mat, not a hot yoga mat. That distinction matters.

Thickness and Comfort

It’s 5mm thick, which is good for joint comfort. My knees felt fine during low lunges and kneeling poses. The cushioning is decent for the price. Not plush, but not like lying on concrete either.

Durability

After about 20 classes, I saw wear. The surface started peeling in one corner where my foot grips during chair pose. The color faded slightly. For a $25 mat, that’s acceptable. Expect to replace it every 6-12 months with regular hot practice.

The Verdict on Gaiam

It’s a fine starter mat. If you’re new to yoga and not sure you’ll stick with it, save your money. But if you’re serious about hot yoga, you’ll outgrow this mat fast. The slip factor alone is a dealbreaker for me.

“I used this mat for 3 months. Switched to Lululemon and never looked back. The Gaiam is fine for stretching at home, but it’s dangerous on a sweaty studio floor.” — Sarah, hot yoga instructor

Lululemon The Mat 5mm Review

Price: $98-$128 | Rating: 4.5/5 | Best for: Serious hot yoga practitioners

Lululemon The Mat is the gold standard for hot yoga. It’s pricey, but it earns every dollar. Here’s the deep dive.

Grip Performance

This is where the magic happens. The top layer is polyurethane, which is naturally grippy when wet. In fact, it gets more sticky as you sweat. I tested this in a 105°F room with 40% humidity. My hands stayed planted in downward dog, even when sweat was dripping off my nose.

The grip is almost too good at first. You’ll feel stuck to the mat, which takes a few classes to get used to. But once you do, you’ll never want to go back. This is the biggest differentiator between the two mats.

Thickness and Comfort

The 5mm version offers excellent cushioning. The natural rubber base adds a slight bounce that feels amazing on your joints. I did a 90-minute Bikram class and my knees felt fine afterward. The 3mm version is available if you want more ground feel, but I recommend the 5mm for hot classes.

Durability

This mat is built to last. After 3 months of heavy use (5+ classes per week), mine looks almost new. The PU surface resists peeling and flaking. The rubber base hasn’t degraded. I’ve seen people use these mats for 3+ years with proper care.

One caveat: you need to clean it properly. The open-cell PU absorbs sweat, so you should wipe it down after every class. If you don’t, it’ll smell. I use a spray bottle with water and a drop of tea tree oil. Takes 30 seconds.

The Verdict on Lululemon

This is the best hot yoga mat I’ve tested under $150. The grip is unmatched, the durability is excellent, and the comfort is top-tier. The only downside is the price and the maintenance. But if you practice hot yoga regularly, it’s worth every penny.

“I’ve had my Lululemon mat for 2 years. It still grips like day one. I’ve tried cheaper mats and they all feel like trash now.” — Mike, vinyasa practitioner

Manduka PRO Yoga Mat

If you want a mat that outlives you, this is it.

Alo Warrior Mat

Price: $88-$98 | Rating: 4/5 | Best for: Style and grip balance

Alo’s Warrior Mat uses a similar PU top layer to Lululemon. It grips well when wet and comes in nicer colors. The 5mm thickness feels similar. The downsides: it’s slightly heavier and the grip fades a bit faster than Lululemon after 6 months. Still a great option if you want something less common.

Jade Yoga Harmony Mat

Price: $75-$100 | Rating: 3.5/5 | Best for: Natural rubber lovers

The Jade Harmony is made from natural rubber and has excellent dry grip. Wet grip is decent but not as strong as PU mats. It’s 5mm thick and has a nice texture. The downside: natural rubber absorbs sweat and smells. You’ll need to wash it weekly. It also breaks down faster than synthetic mats. Good for the eco-conscious, but not as durable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a Gaiam mat for hot yoga?

Technically yes, but you’ll slip. The PVC surface doesn’t handle sweat well. If you’re a light sweater and take gentle classes, you might be okay. For intense hot yoga, invest in a PU-top mat.

How do I clean my Lululemon mat?

Mix water with a few drops of mild dish soap or tea tree oil in a spray bottle. Spray and wipe with a microfiber cloth after every class. Avoid soaking it — the rubber base doesn’t like excess moisture.

Is the Lululemon mat worth the money?

If you practice hot yoga 3+ times a week, yes. The grip difference is night and day. If you’re casual, a cheaper mat from Gaiam might be fine. But expect to replace it more often.

What’s the best thickness for hot yoga?

5mm is the sweet spot. It cushions your joints without sacrificing balance. 3mm is too thin for most people. 6mm is nice but heavier.

Do I need a yoga towel with these mats?

With Lululemon or Alo, no. The PU surface handles sweat directly. With Gaiam, yes — a towel is essential unless you want to slide around. Manduka also benefits from a towel during the break-in period.

Bottom Line

Here’s the short version: if you’re serious about hot yoga and you sweat like I do, buy the Lululemon The Mat 5mm. It costs 4x more than the Gaiam, but it grips better, lasts 3x longer, and makes your practice safer and more enjoyable. The Gaiam is a fine budget mat for stretching at home, but it’s not built for hot, sweaty conditions. If you want something in between, try the Alo Warrior — it gives you 90% of the Lululemon experience for about $20 less. Don’t skimp on your mat. Your knees, your balance, and your confidence in class will thank you.
