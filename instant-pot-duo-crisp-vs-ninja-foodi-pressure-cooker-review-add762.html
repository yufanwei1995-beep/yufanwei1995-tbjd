Two Air Fryer Pressure Cookers, One Winner

You want one appliance that pressure cooks a pot roast and air fries crispy wings without cluttering your counter. The Instant Pot Duo Crisp and Ninja Foodi are the two big names fighting for that spot. I've spent weeks testing both — cooking ribs, chicken wings, hard-boiled eggs, and even donuts — to figure out which one actually earns its cabinet space.

This review is for anyone tired of reading vague comparisons. I'll give you exact cook times, real temperature readings, and the annoying quirks each machine has. By the end, you'll know exactly which one fits your kitchen.

Quick Summary:
  • Who this is for: Home cooks who want to replace a slow cooker, pressure cooker, and air fryer with one countertop appliance.
  • Our top pick: Ninja Foodi 8-Quart (model OL601) — better crisping, easier cleanup, and more consistent temperature control.
  • Price range: $129–$249 depending on size and model. Both regularly go on sale.

What to Look For in a Pressure Cooker + Air Fryer Combo

Before diving into the two heavyweights, here's what actually matters when you're shopping in this category. Ignore the marketing fluff and focus on these five things.

1. The Lid Design Changes Everything

The Instant Pot Duo Crisp has a separate air fryer lid that you physically lift off and store. The Ninja Foodi has a single lid that flips up on a hinge. This sounds minor until you're juggling a hot lid while trying to flip wings. The hinged design on the Foodi wins for convenience. The Instant Pot's detachable lid takes up drawer space and adds a step to every air fry session.

2. Temperature Accuracy Matters for Crisping

Air frying is just convection cooking. If the machine runs 25 degrees cooler than it claims, your food comes out soggy. I tested both with an oven thermometer. The Ninja Foodi stayed within 5 degrees of its set temperature. The Instant Pot Duo Crisp ran about 15–20 degrees low on the "Crisp" setting at 400°F. That difference translates to extra cook time and less crunch.

3. Pot Capacity vs. Basket Capacity

Both machines use the same pot for pressure cooking and air frying. That means the air fry basket sits inside the pot, which reduces usable height. In the 8-quart models, you can fit about 2 pounds of wings in a single layer. Anything more and you're overcrowding, which kills crispiness. The 6.5-quart Instant Pot Duo Crisp gives you even less room — roughly 1.5 pounds of wings max.

4. Pressure Cooking Performance Is Not Equal

Both hit 12 psi and cook beans, rice, and meat well. But the Ninja Foodi has a "Steam & Crisp" mode that pressure cooks first, then automatically switches to air frying. This is a game-changer for foods like chicken thighs — you get juicy meat and crispy skin without transferring anything. The Instant Pot requires you to pressure cook, release pressure, then swap lids and air fry. It works, but it's two hands-on steps instead of one.

5. Cleanup Is a Dealbreaker

The Ninja Foodi's pot and crisper basket are nonstick and dishwasher-safe. The Instant Pot Duo Crisp's air fry basket is also nonstick, but the lid has more crevices where grease collects. The Foodi's simple flip lid wipes clean faster. If you hate scrubbing, this matters more than you think.

Instant Pot Duo Crisp 8-Quart (Model 140-3010-01)

The Duo Crisp is Instant Pot's answer to the Foodi. It combines the classic 11-in-1 pressure cooker with a separate air fryer lid. The 8-quart version is the most popular, and it's often $50–$80 cheaper than the Ninja Foodi equivalent.

Pressure cooking: This is where Instant Pot shines. It's the gold standard for a reason. Beans come out creamy, tough cuts of meat shred easily, and the yogurt function is genuinely useful if you make your own. The stainless steel inner pot is more durable than the Foodi's nonstick coating, and it's also dishwasher-safe.

Air frying: The separate lid works, but it's underwhelming. The fan is less powerful than the Foodi's, and the lower temperature accuracy means you'll need to add 3–5 minutes to most recipes. Frozen french fries come out edible but not great — more like a convection oven than a true air fryer. You'll also have to store that extra lid somewhere, which is annoying in a small kitchen.

The "two lids" problem: There's no automatic venting when you switch between modes. You have to manually release pressure, remove the pressure lid, and attach the air fryer lid. It's a clunky workflow that takes about 30 seconds longer than the Foodi's single-lid design.

Verdict: Buy this if you're on a budget and do more pressure cooking than air frying. The price is right, and the pressure cooking is flawless. Just don't expect great crisping.

Ninja Foodi 8-Quart (Model OL601)

The Foodi is the direct competitor, and it's the one I recommend to most people. The single flip lid is the standout feature — it seamlessly transitions from pressure cooking to air frying without any extra parts.

Steam & Crisp mode: This is the killer feature. You can pressure cook chicken thighs for 10 minutes, then flip the lid and air crisp them for 5 minutes. The result is perfectly juicy meat with crackly skin. You can't do this on the Instant Pot without physically switching lids. The TenderCrisp mode works the same way for roasts — it's a genuine time-saver.

Air frying quality: It's noticeably better than the Duo Crisp. The fan pushes more air, and the temperature holds true. I made a batch of frozen mozzarella sticks that came out golden and crispy in 8 minutes — the Instant Pot took 12 minutes and they were still pale in spots. The 8-quart basket fits about 2.5 pounds of wings in a single layer, which is enough for a family of four.

Pressure cooking: It's very good, but not quite as foolproof as the Instant Pot. The valve can be a bit finicky to seal, and I've had one or two "burn" errors when cooking thick sauces. The nonstick pot is easy to clean but scratches if you use metal utensils — stick with silicone.

Downsides: The lid is heavy, and flipping it up while the machine is hot sends steam right toward your face. It's also taller than the Instant Pot, so it might not fit under low cabinets. And it's more expensive — usually $30–$50 more than the Duo Crisp.

Verdict: Buy this if you want the best all-around machine. The crisping quality and one-lid design justify the higher price.

Instant Pot Duo Crisp 6.5-Quart (Model 140-3001-01)

If you're cooking for one or two, the smaller Duo Crisp is worth considering. It's cheaper, lighter, and takes up less counter space. But there's a catch: the air fryer basket is significantly smaller, which limits what you can crisp.

Who it's for: Singles, couples, or anyone with limited storage. The 6.5-quart pressure cooker is still big enough for a 4-pound chicken or a batch of chili. The air fryer basket fits about 1 pound of wings — that's 6–8 wings, enough for one hungry person.

Performance: It has the same temperature accuracy issues as the 8-quart model. The smaller fan means you'll need to shake the basket more often for even crisping. I found that halving recipes and cooking in smaller batches gave the best results.

The value play: This often drops to around $99 during sales, making it an impulse buy for anyone curious about combo cookers. At that price, it's the best budget option in this category. Just know what you're giving up in air fryer capacity.

Ninja Foodi 6.5-Quart (Model OP301)

This is the smaller sibling of the OL601. It has the same flip lid and Steam & Crisp technology, but in a more compact footprint. It's a solid middle ground if you want the Foodi's features without the bulk.

Performance: Identical to the 8-quart model in terms of temperature accuracy and crisping power. The basket is smaller, so you'll cook in batches for a family. I made a full meal of air-fried salmon fillets and roasted potatoes — everything came out evenly cooked.

The tradeoff: You lose about 1.5 quarts of pressure cooking capacity compared to the 8-quart model. That means a whole chicken fits, but a large pork shoulder won't. If you regularly cook for more than four people, skip this one.

Price: It's usually $20–$30 less than the 8-quart Foodi, but honestly, the price difference is small enough that I'd recommend the bigger model for most buyers. The extra capacity is worth the counter space.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I air fry and pressure cook at the same time?

No. Both machines only do one function at a time. The Ninja Foodi's Steam & Crisp mode is automated — it pressure cooks first, then switches to air frying — but it's still sequential, not simultaneous.

Which one makes better french fries?

The Ninja Foodi, by a clear margin. The higher fan speed and accurate temperature mean you get a crispier exterior without a soggy center. The Instant Pot Duo Crisp produces fries that are more like oven-baked — fine, but not crave-worthy.

Can I use the air fryer lid on the Instant Pot for dehydrating?

Yes. The Duo Crisp has a dehydrate function that works well for jerky and fruit leather. The Ninja Foodi doesn't have a dedicated dehydrate mode, though you can approximate it at low temperatures.

Which is easier to clean?

The Ninja Foodi. The nonstick pot and basket wipe clean quickly, and the flip lid has no crevices for grease to hide. The Instant Pot's detachable lid has more nooks and crannies that trap oil.

Are these worth the counter space?

Only if you'll actually use both functions. If you only pressure cook, buy a standard Instant Pot — it's cheaper and smaller. If you only air fry, buy a dedicated air fryer. The combo makes sense when you want to replace two appliances with one.

The Bottom Line

Buy the Ninja Foodi 8-Quart (OL601) if you want the best overall machine. It crisps better, has a more convenient lid, and the Steam & Crisp mode genuinely saves time. Pay the extra $30–$50 — it's worth it. Buy the Instant Pot Duo Crisp 8-Quart only if you're on a tight budget or you care more about pressure cooking than air frying. Its stainless steel pot is more durable, and the price is hard to beat. For anyone cooking for one or two, the smaller models work, but you'll be frustrated by the limited air fryer capacity. Skip those and get the 8-quart versions — the extra room is worth the counter space.
