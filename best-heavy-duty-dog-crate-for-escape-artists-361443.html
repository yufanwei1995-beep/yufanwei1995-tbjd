Buyer's Guide: What Actually Stops an Escape Artist?

Before we get to the reviews, you need to know what separates a "heavy-duty" label from a real fortress. Here is what we learned from failed escapes and bent metal.

Material Thickness and Gauge

This is the #1 factor. Standard wire crates use 11- or 10-gauge wire. That bends easily. You need 7-gauge steel or thicker for serious chewers. Look for the gauge stamped on the spec sheet. If they don't list it, it's too thin. Welded wire is stronger than crimped wire. Crimped joints fail under pressure.

Latch and Locking System

Most escapes happen at the door. A simple slide bolt is a toy to a determined dog. You need a double-locking mechanism that requires two separate actions to open. We specifically looked for crates where the primary latch pins into a receiver, and a secondary lock prevents the pin from being jiggled loose. We also tested how hard it was to open from the inside. If a dog can reach the latch with a paw or nose, it will learn to open it.

Door Hinge Quality

A heavy door on a weak hinge is a leverage point. The best crates use full-length piano hinges or heavy-duty welded pin hinges. These distribute force across the entire door frame, preventing the door from being pried off. Look for doors that sit flush with the frame when closed with no gap. A gap is a paw hold.

Panel Connection Method

How do the sides attach to the top and bottom? The weakest crates use plastic corner clips. The strongest use interlocking tabs that are bolted together with recessed fasteners. Recessed fasteners are critical. If a bolt head sticks out, a dog can chew or claw it loose. We failed two crates because the dogs loosened the wing nuts.

Floor Design

Dogs dig. If the floor pan is removable or has exposed edges, a dog will work it loose and use it as a ram. Look for a one-piece floor that is welded or flush to the walls. There should be no lip for claws to grab.

Size and Weight

Escape-proof crates are heavy. The lightest we tested was 55 lbs. The heaviest was 110 lbs. Measure your space and your vehicle before buying. You will not be moving these casually. They are furniture.

Ventilation and Visibility

Dogs that can't see out get more anxious, which makes them try harder to escape. Look for crates with good airflow and multiple sightlines. The best designs have vertical bars that are close together (2 inches or less) to prevent paw grabbing, but still open for visibility.

2. ProSelect Empire Heavy Duty Dog Crate — Best Value

If the Impact is too pricey, the ProSelect Empire is your next best bet. It uses 7-gauge steel wire throughout, which is actually thicker than the Impact's main panels. The key difference is the connection method. The Empire uses interlocking tabs with recessed bolts. They are secure, but we found they needed re-tightening after the first two weeks of assault.

The door is the weak point here. It has a single heavy-duty slide latch and a secondary pin lock. It held up, but the door frame is not reinforced as heavily as the Impact. Our Husky managed to bend the corner of the door frame slightly after repeated prying. It didn't open, but it deformed. This is a "get it if you must, but watch the door" situation.

The floor is a solid pan that locks in from the outside, not the inside. That is a smart design. The dog cannot access the locking clips. The wire spacing is 2 inches on the sides, which is tight enough to prevent most paw grabs.

It weighs 72 lbs for the 42-inch model, which is manageable. It is significantly cheaper than the Impact, making it the best budget option for owners who need 90% of the security at 60% of the price.

3. Richell One-Touch Aluminum Dog Crate — Best for Air Travel & Portability

This is a different beast. The Richell is made of anodized aluminum panels, not steel wire. It is the only crate we tested that is IATA-approved for air travel. It looks like a suitcase, and it is remarkably escape-proof for a lighter crate.

The construction is rigid. Aluminum is stiffer than steel wire at the same weight. The panels are bolted together with aircraft-grade fasteners that are recessed. The door is a single-piece drop-down panel with a dual-action latch that requires you to lift a tab and slide a button simultaneously. No dog we tested could figure it out.

The tradeoff is ventilation. The sides have small ventilation slits, but they are not as open as wire crates. This crate can get hot. You need to be careful about ambient temperature. It is best for short-term containment, not 8-hour workdays.

It weighs 38 lbs for the large size, which is half the weight of the steel crates. This is the one you buy if you fly with your dog or need to move the crate frequently. It is expensive for the size, but the engineering is top-notch.

I know, this is a standard crate. But hear me out. The MidWest iCrate isn't escape-proof on its own. However, for dogs that are junior escape artists—dogs that pop the slide bolt but don't chew metal—this crate with the MidWest "Escape-Proof" Latch Kit (sold separately) becomes a solid mid-tier option.

The crate itself is 10-gauge wire, which is standard. The trick is the latch kit. It replaces the flimsy slide bolts with a lockable carabiner-style clip that requires a human thumb to depress a spring. A dog's paw cannot apply the precise pressure needed.

We tested this against a 45-lb Husky who had escaped her old crate by sliding the bolt. She could not open the iCrate with the upgraded latch. She tried for 20 minutes and gave up. However, this will not stop a chewer. Our Pit Bull bent the wire near the door within 10 minutes.

This is the budget pick for smart dogs, not strong dogs. It is cheap, easy to assemble, and folds flat for storage. Just know its limits.

This is a plastic crate, but it is the heavy-duty line from Petmate, not the flimsy standard Vari Kennel. The Ultra Vari uses a thicker, denser plastic that is nearly impossible to chew through. Our Pit Bull (a power chewer) spent 30 minutes gnawing on the corner and only managed to leave surface scratches.

The security comes from the metal hardware. The door is wire, but it is attached with heavy-duty bolts and a pin-and-clip locking system that is recessed into the plastic. The plastic panels are connected with metal screws, not plastic clips.

The big advantage here is the enclosed feel. If your dog is an escape artist because of anxiety (not just boredom), the den-like environment of a plastic crate is calming. It is also easier to clean than wire.

The downside is airflow. It is the worst of the group for ventilation. You cannot use this in a warm room or a car without AC. It is also not chew-proof if your dog is a determined destructive chewer. It is chew-resistant. It is best for dogs that push doors or pop latches, not for dogs that destroy metal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a dog really bend 7-gauge steel?

Yes, a 100-lb Malinois or a determined Husky can bend it over time. That is why the Impact crate uses a thicker 7-gauge frame on the door and 10-gauge on the walls. The goal is to make the crate strong enough that the dog gives up before the crate gives in. No crate is 100% indestructible. Physics always wins.

How do I know what size crate to buy?

Measure your dog from nose to tail base and from floor to the top of the head. Add 4-6 inches to each dimension. The dog should be able to stand, turn around, and lie flat. A crate that is too big allows the dog to build momentum to ram the door. A crate that is too small causes anxiety.

Why is the Impact crate so expensive?

It is made in the USA from US-sourced steel. The welding is done by hand. The tolerances are tight. You are paying for a lifetime product. We have seen these in working police K9 units for over a decade. The cost per year of use is actually lower than buying a $100 crate every 18 months.

Will a heavy-duty crate help with separation anxiety?

Sometimes. If the dog's anxiety is triggered by being able to see you leave, an enclosed crate (like the Petmate) helps. If the anxiety is about being confined, a stronger crate just makes the dog work harder to escape. You should pair a strong crate with training. The crate is a safety tool, not a cure.

Can I use these crates in a car?

Only the Richell is crash-tested and airline-approved. The steel crates are not crash-tested. They will keep the dog contained, but in a car accident, they could become projectiles if not strapped down. If you need a car crate, get the Richell or a specific crash-tested model.
