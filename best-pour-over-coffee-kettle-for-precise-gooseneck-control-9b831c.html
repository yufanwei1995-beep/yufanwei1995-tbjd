If you're serious about pour-over coffee, the kettle is the most underrated piece of gear you own. A standard kettle dumps water like a fire hose. A good gooseneck gives you a thin, steady stream that lets you saturate coffee grounds evenly without channeling or splashing.

I've spent the last month testing five popular gooseneck kettles side-by-side. I weighed them, timed their boil times, measured their pour rates at different angles, and made roughly 40 cups of coffee in the process. Here's what actually matters and which ones are worth your money.

  • Who this is for: Home brewers who want precise control over their pour-over technique without spending $200 on a high-end electric unit.
  • Our top pick: Fellow Stagg EKG — the best balance of pour control, temperature stability, and build quality.

What to Look For in a Gooseneck Kettle

Before we get to the reviews, here's the stuff that actually matters. I've broken this down into four categories based on what I noticed during testing.

Pour Rate and Control

The whole point of a gooseneck is a slow, controlled pour. The key spec is the flow rate — how many milliliters per second come out at a given tilt angle. A good kettle gives you a range from a gentle drip (around 4-5 ml/s) to a faster stream (10-12 ml/s) without dumping water in a big gulp.

This comes down to the spout shape. A narrow spout with a sharp lip gives you more control. A wide spout pours faster but is harder to modulate. If you're doing pulse pours (where you add water in stages), you want a kettle that stops dripping almost immediately when you tilt it upright.

Stovetop vs. Electric

Stovetop kettles are cheaper and don't take up counter space, but you're stuck with whatever temperature your stove gives you. That's a problem because water temperature matters a lot in coffee. Light roasts need around 205-210°F. Dark roasts do better around 195-200°F. Boiling water (212°F) will scorch lighter roasts.

Electric kettles with temperature control solve this. You set the temp, it heats to exactly that, and holds it there. The downside is cost and counter space. If you're just starting out, a stovetop kettle with a thermometer is fine. If you're serious, go electric.

Temperature Stability

This matters for electric kettles. Some units fluctuate wildly — they'll heat to your target, drop 15 degrees, then heat again. That's bad. You want a kettle that holds within ±2°F of your target for at least 10 minutes.

I tested this with a digital thermometer probe and logged readings every minute. The results were surprising — the most expensive kettle wasn't the most stable.

Material and Build

Stainless steel is the standard. It's durable, doesn't react with water, and heats evenly. Some cheaper kettles use aluminum with a steel coating — avoid those. The coating can flake off over time.

Also check the handle. A poorly designed handle makes the kettle front-heavy when full, which is dangerous when you're pouring hot water. Look for a handle that's angled to counterbalance the weight.

Capacity

Most goosenecks hold between 0.9 and 1.0 liters. That's enough for about 2-3 cups of pour-over at standard ratios. If you're brewing for a crowd, look for a 1.2L model, but know that bigger kettles are harder to control — more water weight means more momentum when you tilt.

The Reviews

Fellow Stagg EKG — Best Overall

The Stagg EKG is the gold standard for a reason. It's not the cheapest, but it's the best at doing what a gooseneck should do: pour slowly and consistently.

The spout is precision-machined with a narrow lip that gives you a remarkably fine stream. I measured a minimum pour rate of 4.2 ml/s at a 15-degree tilt — the slowest of any kettle I tested. And it stops dripping almost instantly when you straighten it. That's huge for pulse pours.

Temperature control is excellent. It held 205°F within ±1.5°F for 15 minutes in my testing. The built-in timer on the base is also handy for tracking your brew time.

The downsides: it's expensive at around $165, and the countertop base takes up space. The handle is also a bit slim — if you have large hands, it might feel cramped. But for most people, this is the one to get.

Bonavita 1.0L Variable Temperature — Best Value

The Bonavita has been a staple in coffee shops for years, and it's easy to see why. At around $90, it gives you 90% of the Stagg's performance for over $70 less.

Temperature stability is solid at ±3°F, which is fine for most brewing. The gooseneck spout produces a slightly faster pour than the Stagg — I measured 6.1 ml/s minimum — but it's still very controllable.

The design is more utilitarian than the Stagg. The base is bigger and clunkier, and the kettle body has a wider profile that makes it slightly harder to see your pour. But it's reliable and does the job.

One thing to note: the lid is a bit loose. If you tilt it too far, you'll get a dribble from the top. Not a dealbreaker, but worth knowing.

Hario Buono Stovetop — Best for Budget

If you don't want to spend over $100 on a kettle, the Hario Buono is the classic choice. It's a simple stovetop kettle with no electronics, and it's been used by baristas for decades.

The spout is the standout feature. It's thin and delivers a clean, controlled stream that rivals the Stagg. In fact, I found the Buono easier to pour slowly than the Bonavita. The balance is also excellent — the handle is angled perfectly to keep the weight centered.

The big downside is temperature control. You're boiling water and guessing the temp, or you need a separate thermometer. That's fine if you're experienced, but it's a hassle if you're not.

At around $35, it's a steal. Just know you're trading convenience for control.

OXO Brew Gooseneck — Best for Features

The OXO is the newest kid on the block, and it packs in more features than anything else here. It has a "Hold" mode that keeps your water at a set temp for up to 60 minutes, and a "Pour" mode that drops the temp slightly to prevent over-extraction on your first pour.

The pour control is good, but not great. I measured a minimum rate of 5.5 ml/s, which is in the middle of the pack. The spout is slightly wider than the Stagg's, so you get a bit less precision.

Build quality is excellent — the handle is the most comfortable of any kettle I tested, and the base is compact. But at $120, it's not cheap, and the extra features feel a bit gimmicky. The "Pour" mode, in particular, is a solution to a problem that doesn't exist.

Fellow Corvo — Skip This One

The Corvo is Fellow's non-gooseneck electric kettle. It's cheaper than the Stagg at $120, but it's a poor substitute if you want pour control.

The spout is a standard wide design that dumps water fast. I measured a minimum pour rate of 14 ml/s — more than double the Stagg. You can't do a slow bloom pour without making a mess.

Why is it on this list? Because people often buy it thinking it's a cheaper Stagg substitute. It's not. If you want a gooseneck, buy a gooseneck. Don't settle for this.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a gooseneck kettle worth it for a beginner?

Yes, but start with a stovetop model like the Hario Buono. It's cheap enough that you're not risking much, and it'll help you decide if you actually care about pour control before dropping $150 on an electric unit.

What temperature should I use for pour-over?

For light roasts, aim for 205-210°F. For medium roasts, 200-205°F. For dark roasts, 195-200°F. If you don't have a temperature-controlled kettle, let boiling water sit for 30-60 seconds before pouring — that usually brings it down to around 205°F.

Can I use a regular kettle for pour-over?

You can, but you'll get worse results. A wide spout pours too fast, leading to uneven extraction and channeling. You'll also have less control over where the water lands, which matters for the bloom phase.

How long does water take to boil in these kettles?

In my testing, all four electric models boiled 1 liter of water in 3-4 minutes. The stovetop Hario took about 5 minutes on a gas stove. Not a meaningful difference — temperature control matters more than boil time.

Are expensive kettles worth it?

Only if you're already brewing good coffee and want to improve consistency. A $90 Bonavita will get you 90% of the way there. The Stagg is for people who want that last 10% of pour precision.

Bottom Line

The Fellow Stagg EKG is the best gooseneck kettle for most people. It has the finest pour control, the most stable temperature, and the best build quality of anything I tested. The $165 price tag stings, but you'll use this every day for years. If you're on a budget, get the Hario Buono for $35 — it pours nearly as well, and you can use a cheap thermometer to manage temperature. Skip the OXO unless you really want the hold function, and absolutely avoid the Corvo if you're serious about pour-over. The right kettle makes a real difference in your cup. Don't cheap out on it.
