What to Look For When Buying a Searing Pan for Induction

Induction changes the game. Unlike gas or electric coils, induction heats the pan directly with a magnetic field. That means two things: the pan must be magnetic (ferrous), and it heats up way faster than you're used to. Here's what to check before you buy.

1. Flat Bottom (Non-Negotiable)

Induction cooktops require full contact with the glass surface. If the pan has even a slight warp or a rounded edge, it will wobble and heat unevenly. Cast iron skillets are usually flat, but cheap stainless pans often have a "spinning" issue where the bottom is slightly convex. Test it on the counter before you buy – if it spins, skip it.

2. Thickness and Weight (Heat Capacity)

Searing is about transferring heat into the steak fast, not losing it. A thin pan (under 2mm) will cool down instantly when you drop a cold steak on it. You'll get a gray, steamed crust. Look for a pan that weighs at least 3 pounds (1.3 kg) for a 10-12 inch size. The heavier it is, the more energy it stores, and the better your crust.

3. Induction Base (For Stainless)

Not all stainless pans are induction-compatible. Look for a magnetic stainless steel grade (like 430) or a pan with a fully clad base (like All-Clad's tri-ply). Some pans have a "disc" bottom – a steel plate welded on. These work, but they heat unevenly around the disc edges. Full-clad is better.

4. Handle Design

On an induction cooktop, the glass gets hot, but the pan handle is what you'll grab. Cast iron handles get scorching hot – you'll need a silicone sleeve. Stainless steel handles with a hollow core stay cooler. Both need oven-safe handles if you plan on finishing the steak in the oven.

5. Maintenance vs. Performance Tradeoff

Cast iron requires seasoning and is high maintenance (no soap, dry immediately). Stainless steel is easier to clean but sticks like crazy unless you nail the heat. There's no free lunch. Decide which annoyance you'd rather deal with.

The Test: How We Searing Steaks on Induction

We used a 1,800W portable induction burner set to level 8 out of 10 (about 425°F surface temp). Each skillet was preheated for 5 minutes. We used 1-inch thick ribeyes, patted dry, salted, and seared for 90 seconds per side with avocado oil. We measured crust formation, smoke, sticking, and cleanup time.

2. All-Clad D3 Stainless Steel 12-Inch Fry Pan

Price: $150-$180 | Rating: 4.5/5 | Best For: Cleanup ease, oven finishing

This is a classic tri-ply pan (aluminum core sandwiched between magnetic stainless). It's lighter than the Lodge (about 3.5 lbs) but still has enough mass to sear well. The induction response is snappy – it hit searing temp in 2 minutes flat. The handle stays cooler than cast iron, which is a relief.

The crust: Very good, but slightly less even than the Lodge. The edges of the steak were a touch lighter. You need to use the "Leidenfrost test" (water beads dance on the surface) to know when it's hot enough, or the steak will stick. We had one disastrous first attempt that tore the steak.

The downside: Price. And it's not indestructible – warping is possible if you shock it with cold water while hot. Also, the cooking surface is not as non-stick as seasoned cast iron, so you need more oil.

Verdict: Buy this if you hate seasoning cast iron and want a pan you can scrub with soap.

3. Victoria 12-Inch Cast Iron Skillet (Pre-Seasoned)

Victoria is a Colombian brand that makes a slightly lighter cast iron pan (about 7 lbs vs. Lodge's 8 lbs) with a smoother finish. The downside: The handle is longer and thinner, which means it flexes if you try to toss food. Also, the pre-seasoning is thinner than Lodge's; we had to season it twice before it was truly non-stick. The smoother surface also means the seasoning wears off faster.

Verdict: Get this if you want a slightly lighter cast iron with a smoother finish, but be prepared to re-season it more often.

4. Cuisinart MultiClad Pro 12-Inch Skillet (MCP-12N)

Price: $50-$60 | Rating: 3.5/5 | Best For: Budget stainless option

This is the budget-friendly stainless pan. It's fully clad (tri-ply) and induction-compatible, but the base is thinner (about 2.5mm) than the All-Clad. On our induction burner, it heated up fast but unevenly – the center got hotter than the edges by about 40°F. That meant the steak had a darker stripe down the middle and lighter edges.

The crust: Acceptable, but not great. If you're not a perfectionist, it's fine. If you want an even crust, you'll be disappointed.

The downside: It's prone to warping on high heat – ours developed a slight "rocking" motion after a month of use. Also, the handle gets hot faster than the All-Clad.

Verdict: Only buy this if you're on a strict budget and accept that it's a compromise.

Price: $130-$150 | Rating: 4/5 | Best For: Premium, lightweight cast iron

This is the "fancy" cast iron. It's milled to a glassy smooth finish and weighs only 6.5 lbs – significantly lighter than the Lodge. The heat retention is slightly lower than the Lodge due to the thinner walls, but it's still plenty for a good sear. The induction compatibility is perfect; the bottom is dead flat.

The crust: Excellent, with a slight edge over the Lodge in terms of evenness. The smooth surface means less sticking, and it's a joy to flip a steak with one hand.

The downside: Price. It's 4x the cost of a Lodge for about 10% better performance. Also, the smooth finish requires more careful seasoning – you can't just scrub it with soap and water; you need to maintain the seasoning meticulously.

FAQ

Can I use cast iron on a glass-top induction stove?

Yes, but be careful. Cast iron is heavy and can scratch the glass if you slide it. Always lift the pan, don't slide it. Also, the rough bottom of a Lodge can leave micro-scratches over time. Use a paper towel to check for grit before placing it down.

Why does my stainless pan stick?

You didn't preheat it enough. Stainless needs to reach the Leidenfrost point – about 380-400°F. Test with a drop of water: if it sizzles and evaporates, it's not hot enough. If it forms a dancing bead, it's ready. Then add oil and wait 30 seconds before adding the steak.

Which pan smokes less on induction?

Stainless steel smokes less because it has a lower heat capacity and you can control the temp more precisely. Cast iron stores so much heat that even at medium heat, it radiates enough to smoke oil. Use avocado oil (smoke point 500°F) instead of olive oil (375°F) to reduce smoke.

Do I need a special pan for induction?

Yes. Only ferrous (magnetic) pans work. Test with a fridge magnet – if it sticks to the bottom, it works. Most cast iron works. Stainless only works if it's magnetic (some are not). Avoid aluminum or copper unless they have a steel base.

Is cast iron worth the maintenance?

For searing steak, yes. The heat retention is unmatched. But if you only cook once a week, the seasoning will degrade and you'll deal with rust. Consider a stainless pan if you're a casual cook. If you're serious about crust, cast iron is king.

Bottom Line Verdict

For searing steak on an induction cooktop, buy the Lodge 12-Inch Cast Iron Skillet. It costs less than a dinner out, holds heat like a thermal battery, and delivers the most consistent crust of any pan we tested. The smoke and seasoning maintenance are real annoyances, but they're worth it for the results. If you absolutely refuse to deal with cast iron, the All-Clad D3 is your runner-up – it's pricier and needs perfect preheating, but it cleans up in seconds and handles oven finishing beautifully. Skip the Cuisinart budget option unless you're desperate; the uneven heating will frustrate you. And if you want to splurge on cast iron, the Field Company pan is lovely, but it's a luxury, not a necessity. Get the Lodge, preheat it slowly, use avocado oil, and you'll nail the crust every time.
