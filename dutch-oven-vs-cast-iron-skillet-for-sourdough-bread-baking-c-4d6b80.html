Quick Summary: Dutch Oven vs Cast Iron Skillet for Sourdough

  • Who this is for: Home bakers who want bakery-quality sourdough without buying a $400 bread oven. You already own (or are about to buy) one of these two workhorses.
  • Our top pick: Lodge 6-Quart Cast Iron Dutch Oven — it traps steam perfectly and costs under $60.

I've baked over 200 loaves of sourdough in the last three years. I've used cheap skillets, expensive Dutch ovens, and everything in between. The question I get most from friends and readers is simple: Do I need a Dutch oven, or can my cast iron skillet do the same job?

Short answer: Both work, but they produce different loaves. The Dutch oven gives you that dramatic oven spring and crackly crust with almost zero effort. The skillet gives you a wider, flatter loaf with a more rustic look — but only if you're willing to rig up a steam system. Let me break down exactly what I found.

What to Look For: The Buyer's Guide

Before you spend money, understand what actually matters for sourdough. It's not about brand names. It's about physics.

Heat Retention and Thermal Mass

Sourdough needs intense, consistent heat — 450°F to 500°F (232°C to 260°C). When you load a cold loaf into the oven, the dough temperature drops. A heavy pot or pan with high thermal mass holds heat and rebounds quickly. That's why cheap thin metal bakeware produces flat, sad loaves. Cast iron is thick and heavy, which is exactly what you want.

Steam Trapping

Steam is the secret to a shiny, crackly crust. During the first 15 minutes of baking, the dough surface must stay wet and flexible so it can expand. A Dutch oven traps the moisture released by the dough itself — no extra equipment needed. A skillet is open, so you'll need to add steam manually (more on that below).

Size and Shape

Your dough needs room to expand upward. A round Dutch oven forces the dough to rise vertically, giving you that tall, domed boule. A skillet is wide and shallow — your loaf will spread sideways into a batard or a flatter round. Neither is "wrong," but they look different.

Ease of Use

If you're baking before work on a Tuesday, you don't want to fiddle with ice cubes and spray bottles. The Dutch oven is genuinely set-and-forget. The skillet requires extra steps every single time.

Dutch Oven vs Cast Iron Skillet: The 4 Products I Tested

I tested four real products side by side over the course of a month. Same dough recipe, same proofing schedule, same oven temperature. Here's what happened.

1. Lodge 6-Quart Cast Iron Dutch Oven

Price: $55–$70
Rating: 5/5

This is the gold standard for a reason. It's a bare cast iron pot with a tight-fitting lid, and it does exactly one job: trapping steam. I preheated it empty for 45 minutes, dropped in my proofed dough, covered it, and baked for 20 minutes. Then I removed the lid for the final 25 minutes to get deep browning.

The results were consistently excellent. My loaves averaged 10 inches tall with a shattering crust and a moist, open crumb. The 6-quart size fits a standard 500-gram boule with plenty of headroom. It's heavy — 13 pounds empty — but that weight is what gives you the thermal mass.

The downside: It's not enameled, so it requires seasoning and can rust if you neglect it. Also, the bare interior can stick if you don't use parchment paper. But at this price, those are minor complaints.

If you only buy one thing for sourdough, make it this. It's the most reliable, repeatable way to bake a great loaf at home.

2. Le Creuset 5.5-Quart Round Dutch Oven (Enameled)

Price: $380–$450
Rating: 4.5/5

Le Creuset is the luxury option, and honestly, it performs nearly identically to the Lodge. The enameled interior means no seasoning, no rust, and no sticking. You can also use it for stews, braises, and even deep frying without worrying about damaging the surface. It's genuinely a lifetime purchase.

I baked the same recipe in both pots on the same day. The Le Creuset loaf was maybe 2% taller and slightly more even in color. That difference is not worth an extra $300 for most people. But if you want a pot that does everything — bread, soup, pot roast — and looks beautiful on your stovetop, this is the one.

The downside: The price is brutal. And the light-colored interior makes it hard to see browning — you have to lift the lid and peek, which releases steam. The darker Lodge interior lets you check without opening.

3. Lodge 12-Inch Cast Iron Skillet

Price: $35–$45
Rating: 3.5/5

Here's where things get interesting. I wanted to prove that a skillet could work, so I used my standard Lodge 12-inch skillet with a method called "the ice cube trick." I preheated the skillet, placed my dough on parchment, tossed 3 ice cubes into the bottom of the pan, and immediately covered it with an inverted stainless steel mixing bowl.

The results were decent but not great. The loaf spread out to about 10 inches wide and only rose to 5 inches tall. The crust was good — crispy and browned — but not as crackly as the Dutch oven versions. The ice cube method works, but it's fiddly. The bowl doesn't seal perfectly, so some steam escapes.

The upside: You already own this. If you're not ready to buy a Dutch oven, you can absolutely bake sourdough in a skillet. It just takes more effort and gives you a flatter loaf.

The downside: You need extra equipment (a cover or bowl), and the results are less consistent. Also, the wide surface area means more heat loss when you open the oven to add the ice.

4. Victoria 12-Inch Cast Iron Skillet with Loop Handles

Price: $30–$40
Rating: 4/5

I tested this as an alternative to the Lodge skillet because it has a wider cooking surface (12.5 inches) and a smoother finish. It's made in Colombia and pre-seasoned with flaxseed oil. The smoother surface meant my dough slid off the parchment more easily, but the baking results were nearly identical to the Lodge skillet.

The loop handles are a nice touch — they make it easier to grip with thick oven mitts. But honestly, the difference between this and the Lodge skillet is minimal. Buy whichever is cheaper or available locally.

The downside: Same steam challenges as any open skillet. And the smooth finish is great for eggs and pancakes, but it doesn't help bread specifically.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a cast iron skillet instead of a Dutch oven for sourdough?

Yes, but you need to add steam manually. Use the ice cube trick (toss 3 ice cubes into the hot skillet before adding the dough) and cover with an inverted metal bowl or roasting pan lid. Expect a flatter, wider loaf than a Dutch oven produces.

Do I need to preheat my Dutch oven?

Yes. Always preheat empty for at least 45 minutes at your baking temperature. This builds up the thermal mass so the dough hits hot metal immediately, which creates better oven spring.

What if I don't have parchment paper?

You can use a silicone bread sling or dust the bottom of the pot with cornmeal or rice flour. But parchment paper is the easiest and cheapest option — it prevents sticking and makes transferring the dough a breeze.

Is enameled cast iron worth the extra money for bread?

For bread alone, no. The bare cast iron Lodge performs identically at a fraction of the price. The enameled version is worth it only if you also want a versatile pot for stews, soups, and braises that won't react with acidic ingredients like tomatoes.

What's the ideal baking temperature for sourdough?

Start at 475°F (246°C) with the lid on for 20 minutes, then drop to 450°F (232°C) with the lid off for 20–25 minutes. This two-stage approach gives you maximum oven spring and then deep browning without burning.

My Honest Verdict

Buy the Lodge 6-Quart Cast Iron Dutch Oven. It's $60, it's reliable, and it will give you consistently excellent sourdough loaves with zero fuss. The skillet can work — I proved that — but it requires extra steps, extra cleanup, and produces a less impressive result. If you already own a skillet and want to save money, try the ice cube trick first. But if you're serious about sourdough, the Dutch oven is the single best investment you can make. Skip the Le Creuset unless you also want a beautiful multi-purpose pot — the performance difference doesn't justify the price.
