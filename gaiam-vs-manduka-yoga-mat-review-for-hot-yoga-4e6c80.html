Who This Is For and What Problem It Solves

You're tired of sliding around in a puddle of your own sweat during hot yoga. Your hands slip in downward dog, and your feet skid in warrior two. You've heard two names thrown around—Gaiam and Manduka—but you don't know which one actually works when the room hits 95°F with 40% humidity.

This review is for the sweaty yogi who wants a mat that grips when things get slick. I've spent the last two months testing five specific mats from these two brands in actual hot yoga classes—not just theory. I’m talking about real slip tests, real sweat, and real wear and tear.

Here's the short version: Manduka makes the premium, long-lasting workhorses. Gaiam makes the budget-friendly options that are hit or miss in the heat. But the details matter, and I'm going to break down exactly which one you should buy based on your budget and practice style.

Quick Summary

  • Who this is for: Hot yoga practitioners (Bikram, Vinyasa, Power) who need a non-slip surface.
  • Our top pick: Manduka PRO series (the PRO and GRP are the only ones that truly hold up in heat).
  • Price range: $30 for a basic Gaiam to $150+ for a Manduka PRO. You get what you pay for.

Buyer's Guide: What Actually Matters for Hot Yoga

Before we dive into the reviews, you need to understand three things. These are the non-negotiable specs for hot yoga.

1. The Material (Closed vs. Open Cell)

This is the biggest factor. Closed-cell mats (like PVC or rubber) don't absorb sweat. They wipe clean easily but can get slippery when wet. Open-cell mats (like polyurethane) absorb moisture and get grippier as you sweat. That sounds great, but they're high maintenance—you have to wash them frequently or they smell like a wet dog.

2. Grip in Wet Conditions

You can't just look at a mat and know how it grips when wet. Look for mats with a textured surface or those specifically marketed with "wet grip" technology. The Manduka GRP has a special coating. The Gaiam Print Collection is notoriously slippery when wet—avoid that one for hot yoga.

3. Weight and Portability

Hot yoga mats are usually thicker (4-6mm) for cushioning on hard floors. But thicker means heavier. A Manduka PRO weighs 7.5 pounds. If you walk to the studio, that's a burden. Gaiam mats are lighter (2-3 pounds) but they compress and lose support over time.

The 5 Mats We Tested

I tested these mats over 30 classes. I sprayed them with water, used them in actual sweat sessions, and wiped them down. Here’s the honest breakdown.

1. Manduka PRO 6mm — The Gold Standard

Price: $148-$168 | Rating: 4.5/5

The Manduka PRO is the mat you see in every serious studio. It's 6mm thick, made of dense PVC, and weighs a hefty 7.5 pounds. The surface is smooth but has a "lifetime guarantee" that actually means something.

How it performs in heat: Here's the catch. The PRO is not grippy when wet out of the box. It has a slippery film that takes weeks to break in. You have to scrub it with salt water or use a grip towel for the first month. Once broken in, it becomes surprisingly tacky, but it never gets the "sticky" feel of a rubber mat. It relies on your skin friction, not moisture absorption.

The tradeoff: It's heavy. Carrying it to class is a workout in itself. And if you have slippery hands naturally, you might still slide. But it's the most durable mat I've ever owned—I have one from 2019 that still looks new.

Bottom line: Buy this if you want a mat that lasts a decade and you're okay with a break-in period. It's not the best for soaking wet hands, but it's the best overall quality.

2. Manduka GRP (Grip) — The Hot Yoga Specialist

Price: $128-$148 | Rating: 5/5

The GRP is Manduka's answer to sweaty hands. It has a polyurethane top layer over a rubber base. This is an open-cell surface—it absorbs sweat and gets more grippy as you sweat. In my test, this was the only mat where I didn't need a towel at all.

How it performs in heat: Outstanding. The top layer feels almost suede-like. After 20 minutes of dripping sweat, my hands stuck like glue. The downside is that it absorbs everything—including dirt and oil. You must wash it with a mild soap after every few classes. If you don't, it starts to smell like a gym locker.

The tradeoff: It's 4mm thick, which is thinner than the PRO. On a hard studio floor, my knees felt it during kneeling poses. Also, the polyurethane wears off after about a year of daily practice. It's not a "lifetime" mat like the PRO.

Bottom line: If you do hot yoga more than twice a week, this is the one to get. The grip is unmatched, but you must commit to cleaning it.

3. Gaiam Print Collection — The Budget Trap

Price: $22-$30 | Rating: 2/5

This is the mat you see at Target. It's cute, cheap, and thin. It's a 5mm PVC mat with a printed design on top. The print isn't just for looks—it's a layer of ink that ruins the grip.

How it performs in heat: Terrible. The printed surface is slick from day one. Add water or sweat, and it becomes a slip-and-slide. I nearly injured my wrist in a downward dog during testing. The mat also stretches out and loses its shape after about 10 hot classes.

The tradeoff: It's $25. If you're a beginner who isn't sure hot yoga is for you, maybe it's fine to test the waters. But you'll need a towel over it the entire time, which defeats the purpose of a mat.

Bottom line: Skip this for hot yoga. It's a mat for gentle stretching or yoga on carpet, not for sweat. You'll just waste $25 and have to buy a real mat anyway.

4. Gaiam DryGrip — The Better Budget Option

Price: $40-$50 | Rating: 3.5/5

Gaiam's DryGrip line is their attempt to fix the sweat problem. It has a textured, slightly suede-like finish on top of a PVC base. It's 4mm thick and weighs about 3.5 pounds—much lighter than the Manduka PRO.

How it performs in heat: Surprisingly decent. The texture gives you more traction than the Print Collection. It's not as sticky as the Manduka GRP, but it's workable. I only had to adjust my hand placement a few times in a 60-minute class. It wipes clean easily and doesn't smell.

The tradeoff: The grip wears off. After about 3 months of daily use, the textured top layer started to smooth out. It also sheds microfibers when you wipe it down. It's a "good for the price" mat, not a "great" mat.

Bottom line: Best budget pick if you're on a tight budget. It won't last years, but it won't make you slip either.

5. Manduka PROlite — The Travel Compromise

Price: $92-$110 | Rating: 4/5

The PROlite is the PRO's little brother. Same dense PVC material, same lifetime guarantee, but it's 4.7mm thick and weighs just 4 pounds. It's made for people who want the Manduka quality without the heavy backpack load.

How it performs in heat: Similar to the PRO—it needs the break-in period. Once broken in, it grips well but depends on your skin's natural moisture. It doesn't have the "wet grip" magic of the GRP. It's a solid all-rounder for both hot and non-hot yoga.

The tradeoff: It's not as cushioned as the PRO. For hot yoga where you're often on your knees, you might want a thicker mat. But the weight savings are huge if you carry your mat to the studio.

Bottom line: The best choice if you want Manduka durability but don't want to haul a 7.5-pound mat around. It's a compromise, but a smart one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a regular yoga towel instead of buying a new mat?

Yes. A towel (like the Yogitoes) turns any slippery mat into a grippy one. But it adds bulk, and some people find the towel bunching up under their hands annoying. If you already have a towel that works, save your money. If you hate towels, buy the Manduka GRP.

Why does the Manduka PRO feel slippery at first?

New PRO mats have a chemical film left over from the manufacturing process. You need to scrub it with a mixture of warm water and vinegar or salt, then rinse it. It takes 2-3 sessions to fully break in. Some people never break it in because they give up too early.

Is the Gaiam DryGrip safe for people with sensitive knees?

No. At 4mm, it's thin. If you have knee issues, look for the Manduka PRO (6mm) or use a knee pad. The DryGrip is fine for hands and feet, but it won't protect your joints on a hardwood floor.

How do I clean my mat after hot yoga?

For PVC mats (PRO, PROlite, Gaiam): wipe down with a 50/50 water and vinegar spray after class. For the GRP (polyurethane): use a dedicated mat wash or mild soap. Never use harsh chemicals—they break down the grippy surface.

Final Verdict

Here's your decision tree. If you practice hot yoga three or more times a week and hate using towels, buy the Manduka GRP. It's the only mat that grips better as you sweat, and that alone is worth the $130 price tag. If you're on a budget but still want to stop sliding, the Gaiam DryGrip at $45 is the best bang for your buck—just know it won't last a full year. If you want a mat that outlasts your yoga phase and you don't mind a month of break-in, get the Manduka PRO. Avoid the Gaiam Print Collection completely for hot yoga—it's a slip hazard. My personal pick? I use the GRP for hot classes and the PRO for everything else. They serve different purposes, and honestly, that's the ideal setup if you can swing the cost.
